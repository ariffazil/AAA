## 1. Definition (one paragraph)

The orthogonal-axes framework states that **human relational life
spans at least three independent dimensions**, and that a measurement
on one dimension is **never sufficient** to deduce a coordinate on
another. Concretely:

- **Axis A · Sex/Gender.** The body's social-phenotypic reading and
  the social identity formed around it.
- **Axis B · Sexual Orientation.** The direction toward which desire,
  fantasy, and erotic attention naturally orient — including, but not
  limited to, asexual, demisexual, and other non-monosexual spectra.
- **Axis C · Relationship Quality.** The bandwidth, safety, mutual
  recognition, and durability of how two people actually meet over
  time.

The framework's shorthand: $\text{A} \perp \text{B} \perp \text{C}$.

---

## 2. The Orthogonality Claim (precise)

Orthogonality is **not** the claim that the three axes are causally
disconnected. They obviously interact — culture, history, biography
all couple them. Orthogonality is the weaker and more useful claim:

> *The couplings between axes do not define the axes themselves, and
> therefore a measurement on one axis is never sufficient to deduce
> the coordinate of another.*

This is a **categorical / epistemological** claim, not a metaphysical
one. It is the same move that distinguishes *correlation* from
*definition* in any well-formed measurement system.

## 4. Why The Collapse Happens (mechanism)

Two mechanisms produce the collapse:

1. **Narrative compression.** Humans — and language models especially —
   prefer stories with fewer moving parts. Three independent axes is
   harder to tell than "X happened because of Y". So the storyteller
   collapses axes that are statistically correlated in their local
   experience, and the compression looks like a rule.
2. **Cultural inheritance.** Most inherited social scripts are
   organised around a *single* coordinate (e.g. "marriage =
   opposite-sex + lifelong"). Any relational event that breaks the
   script is then re-narrated as "one of the axes must have been
   wrong". The framework is the antidote: each axis stands on its
   own.

The framework is robust against both: it does not need the three axes
to be uncorrelated in the world — only that the model not confuse
correlation with definition.

## 5. What The Framework Does NOT Claim

To forestall over-reading:

- It is **not** a claim that orientation is "just a preference" or
  chosen.
- It is **not** a claim that gender is irrelevant to relational
  experience.
- It is **not** a denial that sexual orientation is often stable,
  early-formed, and identity-level. (That is a separate doctrine.)
- It is **not** a tool for "diagnosing" anyone's orientation from
  their behaviour. Orientation is a first-person coordinate. The
  framework is about how *not* to misread other axes as orientation.

## 6. Diagnostic Use (how to apply)

When a relational situation presents, the framework instructs the
analyst to:

1. **Decompose.** Identify the axis each piece of evidence is
   actually on.
2. **Refuse shortcuts.** Forbid inferences that cross axes without
   explicit warrant.
3. **Mark uncertainty.** When evidence is only on one axis, mark the
   others `UNKNOWN` rather than imputing them.
4. **Default to first-person.** When orientation is in question, the
   default is to ask the person — or, failing that, to leave the
   coordinate `UNKNOWN` rather than back-derive it.

This is operational hygiene. It does not produce answers where there
were none; it prevents answers where there should be none.

Verdict: the failure on C does not imply the orientation on B was wrong *during* the marriage. Orientation can be late-articulated; quality was real-time. Each axis stands.
