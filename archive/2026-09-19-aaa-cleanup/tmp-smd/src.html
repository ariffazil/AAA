Executive Summary 01

In August 2024, a gas-to-liquids plant on the Sarawak coast received two invoices for the same gas, from two different companies, each claiming to be the lawful seller. It was not a billing error. It was the first time a foreign multinational had been handed physical proof of a constitutional fault line that has run under Malaysia since 1974 — and the courts have now spent two years deciding who gets paid without ever deciding who is right.

Shell MDS (Malaysia) Sdn Bhd operates the world's first commercial gas-to-liquids plant, commissioned in Bintulu in 1993. It buys natural gas, converts it into waxes, drilling fluids and middle distillates, and exports to more than 50 countries. It employs around 450 people, nearly 90% of them Sarawakian. For three decades it was a national trophy.

In August 2024 it signed a gas supply agreement with Petroleum Sarawak Berhad (Petros), the state-owned company designated sole gas aggregator under Sarawak's Distribution of Gas Ordinance 2016 — while still holding a 2020 supply agreement with Petroliam Nasional Berhad (Petronas), the federal national oil company whose exclusive nationwide rights come from the Petroleum Development Act 1974.

Two months later, both sent invoices. Shell MDS did what any prudent board would do: it stopped paying and asked a judge who the correct recipient was. What followed was a two-year procedural war in which every court that touched the case reversed the one before it — and in which the underlying question was ultimately declared, by a High Court judge, to be a matter of law so pure that no witness would ever be called.

The Court of Appeal has told Shell MDS to pay Petronas roughly RM70–80 million every month until the High Court finishes deciding who owns the gas. Shell MDS is paying. The question remains open. INTERIM STATE OF PLAY — SEPTEMBER 2026

Then, on 2 September 2026, DealStreetAsia reported that Shell is exploring the sale of a majority stake in Shell MDS — potentially all or part of its 72% — in a transaction valued at around US$1 billion (RM4.05 billion), with Barclays advising. Shell confirmed a "potential strategic review". No decision, it said, has been made.

Eleven days later, Sarawak took formal control of Bintulu Port — the port from which Shell MDS's products and Petronas's LNG both leave the country.

Why this matters beyond one plant

Three facts, held together, describe the whole problem:

  1. RM70–80 million per month is being paid to Petronas without any court having ruled that Petronas is legally entitled to it. The payment rests on an interim appellate order and a signed agreement, not on a decided question of law.
  2. Shell MDS told the court that paying the wrong party could expose it to criminal liability under Sarawak's state ordinance. That claim has not been tested.
  3. The buyer of Shell's 72% will inherit that exposure. Any purchaser is acquiring a business whose single largest cost item — feedstock gas — is subject to a live jurisdictional dispute that its own lawyers say carries criminal risk.

This dossier sets out what is documented, what each party has claimed, what the courts have actually decided, and what remains genuinely unknown. It ends with what it means for the ordinary Malaysian household — because the money in dispute is, in the end, the same money.

The Asset 02

Shell Middle Distillate Synthesis (Malaysia) Sdn Bhd sits at Tanjung Kidurong, Bintulu, Sarawak. It was commissioned in 1993 — the culmination of roughly two decades of Shell research into converting methane into liquid hydrocarbons. It is, by Shell's own description and by the Bintulu Development Authority's, the world's first commercial-scale gas-to-liquids plant.

The process is not refining. It breaks natural gas into synthesis gas — carbon monoxide and hydrogen — then reassembles the molecules into long-chain hydrocarbons that can be cut into different products. Gas in, liquid out.

Read the ownership table twice

Petronas owns 7% of the plant. Sarawak owns 7%. Shell owns 72%. Three of the four owners are also parties or interested parties to the dispute over who may lawfully sell the plant its gas. The only neutral shareholder is Mitsubishi, which has said nothing publicly and has no upstream stake in the argument. A plant that cannot buy gas from a legally certain seller is, for accounting and governance purposes, a plant with an unresolved input risk — and that risk now sits on a sale document.

A plant that has been rebuilt once already

The Bintulu GTL plant suffered a serious explosion in 1997 and was substantially reconstructed between 1998 and 2000. Malaysian investment documentation records a further rejuvenation programme described as the largest investment in the facility since that rebuild, intended to support reliable production for a further two decades. In 2018 Shell marked its 25th anniversary in partnership with the Sarawak state government and the Bintulu Vocational College; in 2024 it marked its 30th.

The point is not sentimental. Shell has put real capital into keeping this plant alive. Firms do not normally sell assets immediately after committing to extend their operating life. When they do, the reason is usually on the other side of the balance sheet — or in the legal environment.

How the Fault Line Opened 03

The dispute did not begin with a lawsuit. It began with a legal instrument that two governments both considered binding, and a company that was told to obey both.

Four documents, one territory

InstrumentYearWhat it says
Oil Mining Ordinance (OMO) 1958 Sarawak's pre-independence oil mining law. Sarawak's position is that it has never been repealed, and therefore coexists with the PDA rather than being displaced by it.
Malaysia Agreement (MA63) 1963 The instrument by which Sarawak and Sabah entered the federation. Predates the PDA by eleven years. Sarawak's constitutional anchor.
Petroleum Development Act (PDA) 1974 Federal law. Vests exclusive rights over petroleum operations nationwide in Petronas. Enacted during a period of emergency, when Parliament was not functioning normally.
Distribution of Gas Ordinance (DGO) 2016 Sarawak state law, amended 2023. Designates Petros as the sole gas aggregator within Sarawak — the entity that buys gas from producers and resells it to users.

Read the years again. The OMO is 1958. MA63 is 1963. The PDA is 1974 — later than both. Sarawak's argument is chronological as well as constitutional: the federal statute that Petronas relies on arrived after the instruments that Sarawak says protect it, and cannot silently extinguish them.

What an interpleader is — and why it matters

An interpleader is a procedural device for a party who holds something (here, money) that two other parties both claim. The holder says, in effect: I do not dispute that I owe this. I dispute only to whom. Let the court decide, and I will pay whoever the court names. It is the procedure of a neutral. By choosing it, Shell MDS placed itself outside the fight between Petronas and Petros — and Petronas's later arguments that Shell MDS "created its own predicament" sit awkwardly beside the fact that Shell MDS is the one party in the case that has never claimed any entitlement to the money.

The criminal-liability claim

The single most consequential assertion in the whole matter is one Shell MDS made in its filings and repeated in reporting: that paying either party without a court determination could expose the company to criminal liability under the DGO 2016. This is what converted a commercial question into a legal emergency. A company can absorb an overpayment. It cannot absorb a prosecution.

That claim, as far as this dossier can establish, has never been adjudicated on its merits.

The Litigation Record 04

Every entry below is drawn from contemporaneous court and business reporting. Where sources conflict, both figures are shown.

06 JAN 2025
Judicial Commissioner Arziah Mohamed Apandi issues grounds of judgment. She records that Shell MDS faces immediate risk of supply disruption under two competing GSAs; that monthly exposure is in the region of RM70–100 million; that Shell MDS offered to set funds aside in an interest-bearing account pending resolution; and that Petronas had proceeded to call on a bank guarantee.
10 JAN 2025
Petronas files a notice of appeal. Petros does not appeal — it lets its 30-day window lapse, meaning Petros does not challenge the injunction at that stage.
28–29 JAN 2025
The High Court grants Shell MDS an interim injunction. Effect: payments to both Petronas and Petros are suspended; Petronas must continue supplying gas; Petronas is restrained from calling on Shell MDS's UOB bank guarantee.

Arziah finds Shell MDS has "an exceptionally and unusually strong and clear case", noting that the company "did not bring itself to be caught in this turmoil", that "it is not the fault of SMDS that the tussle between Petronas and Petros had come about", and that it "is willing and able to make payments to the correct entity as determined by the court."
17 JUL 2025
Petronas asks the Court of Appeal for an early hearing. Counsel Datuk Dr Cyrus Das states Petronas is losing more than RM80 million per month while still supplying the gas. Court reporting records the purported arrears at over RM523 million, with Petros understood to be seeking close to RM600 million. Shell MDS's counsel is Christopher Leong; Petros appears as intervener through Foo Joon Liang. The court also admits the federal–Sarawak joint declaration as evidence.
08 OCT 2025
High Court judge Mahazan Mat Taib rejects Petronas's application to convert the interpleader Originating Summons into a writ. Her reasoning: the dispute is "a matter of federal and state laws. It is a matter of law and not facts." No witness credibility is in issue. A full trial, she says, would delay resolution. Petros and the Sarawak government had opposed Petronas's application; Shell MDS took a neutral stance.
22 SEP 2025
The Court of Appeal — Azimah Omar presiding, with Wong Kian Kheong and Ismail Brahim — sets aside the injunctions. It orders Shell MDS to pay Petronas all outstanding sums for August 2024 to 5 October 2025, by 6 October.

Justice Wong holds that Arziah was wrong to grant the injunctions because there was no breach of agreement by Petronas — the company had continued to supply gas throughout. He finds there is "less risk of injustice" in not compelling Petronas to keep supplying without payment.

Costs: Shell MDS ordered to pay Petronas RM50,000; Petros ordered to pay Petronas RM30,000.

Amount owed including accrued interest: reported as approaching RM1 billion.
05 FEB 2026
The Court of Appeal reconvenes to clarify the wording of its own order — a rare step. Shell MDS's counsel argues the formal order must be precise because Shell MDS holds its stake on behalf of several other companies, and it needs a clear legal instruction before transferring nearly RM1 billion.

Petronas's counsel Khoo Guan Huat confirms all parties agreed to a draft order but disagreed on phrasing — specifically on how interest and future payments are to be handled.

The amended order directs Shell MDS to (a) pay all past and present sums plus late-payment interest under the PETRONAS Gas Supply Agreement 2020, and (b) continue paying Petronas for future supplies under that same agreement — in both cases "subject to the order of the High Court upon the final disposal of the interpleader summons."
ONGOING
The interpleader itself — the actual question of who owns the gas — remains undecided before the High Court in Kuala Lumpur. As at February 2026 no hearing date had been fixed; parties were still exchanging affidavits. The Court of Appeal ordered that the matter be expedited. Shell MDS continues paying Petronas an estimated RM70–80 million per month in the interim.

Separately, Petros has filed its own suit in the Kuching High Court seeking to stop Petronas from using Petros's bank guarantee for gas payments, on the basis that Petronas is not licensed to operate in Sarawak under state law.
A High Court granted relief. The Court of Appeal reversed it. The Court of Appeal then had to amend its own order four months later because the parties could not agree on what it meant. And the question that started it all has still not been argued to judgment. WHAT TWO YEARS OF LITIGATION HAS PRODUCED
The one thing the appeal decided — and how narrowly it decided it

The Court of Appeal did not rule that Petronas is legally entitled to the money. It ruled that Shell MDS had not shown a sufficient basis for an injunction, because Petronas had kept supplying gas and therefore had not breached the 2020 agreement.

That is a procedural conclusion about the conditions for interim relief. It is entirely compatible with Petros ultimately winning the interpleader. A reader who takes "Shell ordered to pay Petronas" as a finding on ownership has read the headline, not the holding. The order says so itself: it operates "subject to the order of the High Court upon the final disposal of the interpleader summons."

How to read a number you cannot verify

This case has generated more large figures than it has judgments. Separate them before repeating any:

  • Court-recorded — RM70–80m per month; RM523m; the costs awards. These appear in reporting of proceedings.
  • Party-claimed — Petronas's ~RM2bn; Petros's ~RM600m; Shell MDS's criminal-exposure assertion. Assertions in filings, untested.
  • Analyst or press estimates — the ~RM1bn total; the US$1bn sale valuation; the RM20bn/yr Petros upside; the US$120–130m EBITDA. Indicative, sourced to unnamed parties or to models.

One further number deserves its own line, because it will be quoted most and verified least. Reports put the value of the stake under review at "around US$1 billion". That appears to be a market estimate of what a controlling interest in a business earning US$120–130 million of EBITDA a year might fetch. No buyer, no agreed price, and no signed transaction exist. Shell's own statement is that a review is under way and no decision has been made. Everything else is inference.

The Exit 07

On 2 September 2026, DealStreetAsia reported that Shell plc is exploring the sale of a majority stake in Shell MDS, in a transaction that could be valued at around US$1 billion, with Barclays appointed as financial adviser. Upstream reported the same on 7 September; Energy Intelligence confirmed on 8 September, describing it as a possible sale of all or part of Shell's 72%. Shell's spokesperson confirmed a "potential strategic review of its equity interest" and added that no decision had been made and that the business continues to focus on safe and reliable operations.

The conventional explanation

Shell under chief executive Wael Sawan has been reallocating capital towards higher-return barrels. In 2026 alone: Sprng Energy, its India renewables business, agreed for US$1.8 billion in July on a stated rationale of "high-grading" the power portfolio; a 50% interest in the BP-operated Na Kika platform plus associated Gulf of Mexico assets agreed for US$1.7 billion in June; and, days before the Bintulu report, completion of the ARC Resources acquisition at approximately US$13.9 billion equity value. Shell is not simply shrinking. It is swapping old assets for new ones.

Set against Shell's two remaining GTL plants, the logic looks sharper still:

PlantCapacityShell stake
Bintulu GTL (Malaysia)~14,700 b/d72%
Pearl GTL (Qatar)~140,000 b/d100%

Pearl is roughly ten times the size and wholly owned. Bintulu is the pioneer — the plant that proved the technology. If Shell continues to believe in GTL, it can hold that belief through Qatar alone. It does not need Bintulu to hold the intellectual position. It needs Bintulu only to hold the volume — and it can buy volume elsewhere.

What the conventional explanation does not cover

Portfolio discipline explains why a company would sell something. It does not explain why this owner would sell this asset now. Three facts sit outside the standard account:

  1. The feedstock dispute is unresolved. Shell MDS is paying RM70–80 million a month to Petronas under an interim order that expressly defers the ownership question. Its own filings assert a criminal-liability exposure if it pays the wrong party. A seller has to disclose that. A buyer has to price it.
  2. The legal position could change against the current payer. The interpleader remains live. If the High Court finds the DGO regime operative, the interim payment arrangement unwinds — and whoever owns the plant inherits the consequences, not the seller.
  3. Shell committed to the plant's life extension. A rejuvenation programme was described as the largest investment since the 1998–2000 rebuild, intended to secure two more decades of production. Selling the majority stake within a period relevant to that horizon is not obviously consistent with the investment case that justified it.
Shell can explain why it sells. It has not yet had to explain why it sells now — and the single new fact of the last two years is that the gas this plant runs on is claimed by two governments. THE QUESTION THE ANNOUNCEMENT DOES NOT ANSWER
Who could buy — and why the buyer's identity is the whole story

Three orders of possibility, each with different consequences for Malaysia:

  • Sarawak or a Sarawak-linked entity. This would move the plant under the same government that licenses its feedstock and has just taken Bintulu Port. Commercially the cleanest resolution — and simultaneously the most significant transfer of a strategic downstream asset in the state's modern history.
  • Petronas. Petronas already owns 7%. Moving to control would settle the supply question by making supplier and operator the same party — and would be read by Sarawak as the federal side consolidating exactly what the dispute is about.
  • A third-party international buyer. New private capital with no stake in the Malaysian constitutional argument — and therefore with no ability to resolve it. Such a buyer would be purchasing the exposure, not the dispute.

No buyer has been announced. No bid is public. Anyone who tells you which of the three it will be is guessing.

The Moves Around the Case 08

A dispute about gas is also a dispute about who controls the ground, the pipes and the ports. In the same months as the litigation, three parallel developments changed the physical map of Sarawak's energy sector.

Bintulu Port passes to Sarawak

Effective 1 September 2026, all assets, rights, interests and obligations of Bintulu Port Holdings (LPB) were transferred and vested in the Sarawak government. The takeover took roughly three years from the Sarawak Premier's initial announcement. On 16 September 2026 — Malaysia Day — the Prime Minister and the Sarawak Premier jointly witnessed the official handover in Kuching.

Bintulu Port is not incidental to this case. It is the maritime outlet for the Bintulu industrial complex: the LNG export facility, the GTL plant and the surrounding industries. Control of the port is control of the export route for products manufactured from gas whose ownership is in litigation.

Shell's earlier exit from Malaysia LNG Tiga

In 2018, Shell Gas Holdings (Malaysia) Limited completed the sale of a 15% shareholding in Malaysia LNG Tiga to the Sarawak State Financial Secretary for an agreed US$750 million, with a net consideration after dividend adjustments of approximately US$640 million. Sarawak's stake in MLNG Tiga rose to 25%. Shell's stated rationale then was, in its own words, "consistent with its strategy to simplify its portfolio and reshape Shell into a simpler, more resilient and focused company."

That is the same rationale — capital discipline, portfolio simplification — being offered for the Shell MDS review eight years later. It is worth noting that the 2018 sale moved a Shell interest in a Malaysian LNG asset into Sarawak's hands. If there is a pattern, it is Shell selling down and Sarawak buying in.

An investor walks away

Reporting in May 2025 recorded that ConocoPhillips had withdrawn from a RM13.7 billion deepwater project off Sarawak's coast, cited alongside the Shell MDS action as evidence that the uncertainty is already affecting investor sentiment.

Shell, for its part, has not left Malaysia. Its reporting describes interests in 20 Malaysian production-sharing contracts with stakes ranging from 20% to 92.5%; operatorship of major gas-producing interests offshore Sarawak; development of the Rosmari-Marjoram field approximately 220 kilometres offshore Bintulu, including a platform, subsea pipeline and new onshore gas plant; and a 30% interest in the Jerun field, which reached first gas in 2024 with capacity of up to 550 million cubic feet per day plus condensate, flowing towards customers in the Bintulu area including Malaysia LNG.

The distinction that matters

A sale of Shell MDS would not mean Shell is abandoning Sarawak. It would mean Shell is distinguishing between an older downstream processing asset and newer upstream and integrated-gas positions. The trouble is that the older asset is the one with the jurisdictional problem, and the newer ones sit upstream of the argument. Shell would be keeping the gas it produces and selling the plant that has been invoiced twice for buying it. On the seller's side that is a rational separation. From the buyer's side, it is a disclosure problem.

Untuk Rakyat Malaysia — Apa Sebenarnya Yang Kita Sedang Bayar ⚒

Ada satu soalan yang lebih penting daripada siapa menang kes ini. Dan ia lebih mudah difahami daripada mana-mana Akta.

Jika gas ini milik Sarawak, kenapa Sarawak tidak dapat harganya?

Jika gas ini milik Persekutuan, kenapa Persekutuan tidak dapat ketenangan?

Bulan lepas seorang Perdana Menteri memberitahu seluruh negara bahawa tiada masalah yang tidak boleh diselesaikan. Bulan ini sebuah syarikat Britain yang telah beroperasi di Sarawak selama lebih seratus tahun memasang papan tanda "untuk dijual" pada loji pertama dunia yang menukar gas menjadi cecair — sementara dua buah kerajaan masih belum bersetuju siapa pemilik bahan mentah yang masuk ke dalamnya.

Dan pada setiap satu daripada tiga puluh hari yang berlalu tanpa penyelesaian, satu syarikat membayar kira-kira RM70 hingga RM80 juta kepada sebuah pihak yang tiada mahkamah pernah mengesahkan sebagai pemilik sah. Wang itu keluar. Wang itu masuk. Dan soalan asal kekal terbuka.

Ini bukan kisah Shell. Ini kisah rutin kita.

Pada 1974, satu undang-undang diluluskan ketika Darurat — ketika Parlimen tidak bersidang secara normal — dan ia memberi satu syarikat hak eksklusif ke atas minyak dan gas seluruh negara, termasuk negeri-negeri yang masuk ke dalam Persekutuan pada 1963 dengan syarat-syarat mereka sendiri yang tertulis.

Selama lima puluh dua tahun, Sarawak menerima lima peratus. Ia tidak pernah dipersoalkan secara rasmi sehinggalah Sarawak membina syarikat sendiri dan mula menghantar bil.

Dan inilah bahagian yang paling penting untuk kita faham:

Isu ini bukan tentang negeri lawan Persekutuan. Sarawak ialah Malaysia. Minyak di perairan Sarawak ialah minyak Malaysia. Apabila kita bercakap tentang "Sarawak menuntut", kita sebenarnya bercakap tentang satu pertiga daripada negara meminta akaun bagi hak yang ia tidak pernah secara bebas melepaskan. Apa yang kita semua tanggung — tidak kira siapa menang

  • Kita tanggung kos ketidakpastian. Apabila dua kerajaan kedua-dua mendakwa hak untuk menjual gas yang sama, syarikat menunggu. Loji ditangguh. Pelabur keluar. ConocoPhillips sudah keluar dari projek bernilai RM13.7 bilion. Setiap pelaburan yang tidak datang ialah kerja yang tidak wujud.
  • Kita tanggung kos subsidi. PETRONAS menyumbang melalui dividen yang membiayai sebahagian besar subsidi dan perkhidmatan awam. Apabila margin PETRONAS terhakis, sama ada subsidi dipotong, cukai dinaikkan, atau perkhidmatan dikurangkan. Tiada jalan keempat yang selesa. Analis sektor sudah memberi amaran bahawa kesan terawal akan dirasai oleh M40 dan B40 melalui harga bahan api dan elektrik.
  • Kita tanggung kos masa. Kes ini bermula November 2024. Kini September 2026. Mahkamah Tinggi masih belum menetapkan tarikh pendengaran untuk soalan sebenar. Dua puluh dua bulan berlalu, dan soalan asal belum dihujahkan sampai ke penghakiman.
  • Kita tanggung kos maruah. Negara yang berjanji kepada Jepun dan Korea bekalan dua puluh hingga tiga puluh tahun lamanya mesti menyerahkan gas itu. Kontrak sedunia itu tidak boleh dipinda kerana kita belum selesai berdebat siapa yang boleh menandatanganinya.

Tiga soalan yang patut ditanya oleh setiap rakyat Malaysia

  1. Kalau dua kerajaan kedua-dua benar, siapa yang sebenarnya salah? Bukan Sarawak. Bukan Persekutuan. Yang salah ialah lima puluh dua tahun tanpa satu formula bertulis yang diterima kedua-dua pihak. Ketidakjelasan itu bukan keadaan cuaca. Ia satu keputusan — dibuat oleh orang, setiap tahun, untuk tidak memutuskan.
  2. Kenapa pertikaian hak milik bahan mentah hanya menjadi kecemasan apabila sebuah syarikat asing menuntut kepastian? Selama berdekad, isu ini diurus sebagai hal politik. Ia menjadi hal undang-undang hanya apabila Shell MDS berkata ia boleh didakwa di bawah undang-undang negeri. Jika ia cukup serius untuk sebuah syarikat Britain, ia cukup serius untuk kita.
  3. Siapa yang akan memiliki loji ini selepas ia dijual — dan adakah dia akan diberi jawapan yang Shell tidak pernah dapat? Kerana itulah sebenarnya yang sedang dijual. Bukan 470,000 tan produk setahun. Bukan 450 pekerja, sembilan puluh peratus daripada mereka anak Sarawak. Yang dijual ialah pendedahan kepada soalan yang tiada sesiapa mahu jawab.

Dan satu perkara yang mesti kita berhenti buat

Kita mesti berhenti bercakap tentang ini sebagai pertandingan antara "Semenanjung" dan "Borneo". Sarawak bukan lawan. Sarawak ialah rumah. Apabila kita bertanya "kenapa Sarawak minta lebih", kita mengulang kesilapan yang sama yang dibuat pada 1974: kita menganggap hak seseorang itu sebagai permintaan, bukan sebagai hak milik.

Sebaliknya, apabila kita bertanya kepada PETRONAS "kenapa tak bagi", kita juga tersalah faham. PETRONAS bukan kerajaan. Ia syarikat yang memegang sesuatu atas nama kerajaan. Ia tidak boleh memberi apa yang kerajaan tidak beri.

Dan kerajaan itu — yang mengambil dividen RM20 bilion tahun ini, yang merupakan pemilik tunggal PETRONAS melalui MoF Inc, yang menandatangani perisytiharan bersama pada Mei 2025 dan kemudian tidak menukarnya menjadi satu perjanjian — perlu menjawab satu soalan sahaja:

Adakah kita lebih rela membayar RM1.5 bilion dan masih tidak selesai, atau menulis satu formula dan benar-benar selesai? Kerana yang pertama kelihatan murah. Tetapi ia dibayar setiap tahun, selama-lamanya, dan ia tidak pernah menutup buku. SOALAN YANG TIADA LAPORAN AKAN JAWAB UNTUK KITA

Penutup

Loji di Bintulu dibuka pada 1993. Ia ialah loji gas-ke-cecair komersial pertama di dunia. Enam tahun kemudian, sekumpulan jurutera daripada loji itu dihantar ke Qatar untuk membantu membina Pearl GTL, yang kini sepuluh kali lebih besar.

Jadi kita pernah membina perkara yang dunia tidak pernah buat. Kita mengajar Qatar. Kita menghasilkan lilin "Sarawax" yang dinamakan sempena negeri ini, menghantarnya ke lebih lima puluh negara, dan ia berakhir di dalam gincu, pakej makanan dan tayar pesawat orang lain.

Tiada apa dalam cerita ini yang menunjukkan kita tidak mampu. Yang ditunjukkan ialah sesuatu yang lebih sukar diakui: kita mampu, dan kita tidak selesai.

Dan perbezaan antara dua keadaan itu — mampu, dan tidak selesai — dibayar setiap bulan, oleh setiap seorang daripada kita, dalam bentuk yang tidak pernah muncul sebagai satu baris dalam mana-mana bajet.

Jika kita sendiri tidak boleh bersetuju siapa pemilik gas kita selepas enam puluh tiga tahun — jangan salahkan sebuah syarikat Britain kerana ia mula bertanya. Satu-satunya kejutan dalam seluruh urusan ini ialah bahawa ia mengambil masa selama ini.
