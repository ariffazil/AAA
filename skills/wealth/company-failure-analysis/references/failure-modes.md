# Failure modes and their detectors

## The classification

| Mode | What changes | Detector | Catches | Blind to |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Erosion** | the distance to the barrier closes over years | distance-to-barrier model | slow multi-year declines | shocks; anything fast |
| **Fabrication** | the distance looks wide while cash never arrives | cash conversion and cash coverage | reported profit not backed by cash | genuine declines with honest books |
| **Shock** | a healthy balance sheet meets a price or demand event | none of the above | — | everything; this is the model's declared limit |
| **Transfer** | the right to the asset moves, legally | none — qualitative | — | everything; no number changes |

## Why fabrication defeats a distance model

Distance to barrier is `ln(V / B)`. When the numerator `V` is the line item being falsified,
inflating it **widens** the apparent distance. The firm therefore looks safer exactly as its books
diverge from reality.

Signature to look for: a distance reading that stops closing — or reverses to widening — at the
same time an auditor qualification, restatement or regulatory inquiry appears. That coincidence is
the tell. The distance model is not malfunctioning; it is reading the fabricated input faithfully.

The detector with no such blind spot is **cash conversion**: operating cash flow divided by reported
profit. A genuine business converts at or above roughly 1.0 over time. Sustained values below 0.5 —
and especially below 0.3 — mean reported profit is not arriving as money. Pair it with cash divided
by total borrowings, where the signal is the **monotone trend**, not the level: a sequence falling
every single year without a step change is a firm running out of the ability to service its own debt
while still reporting profit.

## Why transfer defeats both

No default occurs. No book is falsified. The arithmetic stays valid throughout, because it assumed
the entity held the right to what it was measuring, and that assumption is the thing being
renegotiated.

Observable surface: **who is permitted to invoice**. Two parties issuing invoices for the same
underlying supply, a right moved under a different legal jurisdiction, or an entitlement reassigned
downward are all transfer signals. None of them appear in total assets or total liabilities.

Investigate, in this order:
1. Who holds the right, on what instrument.
2. Which jurisdiction and which courts govern it.
3. Who has actually been invoicing, and who has been receiving payment.
4. Whether the entity's own claim of exclusivity has been tested in a court and survived.

A court win on the right, alongside a shrinking entitlement, is a common and coherent combination:
the legal position holds while the commercial position erodes. Report both; do not let the win
suppress the erosion.

## Shock — the declared limit

A firm with a widening distance and steady cash can still fail, because a price or demand event
arrives. Detecting shocks is not a modelling problem; it is a different problem. State this as an
explicit limit rather than quietly crediting the model with cases it did not catch.

When reporting a validation panel, name the misses and their cause. Misses caused by shock and
misses caused by a too-slow rate of change are different failures of the method and should not be
lumped together.

## Diagnostic order

1. Compute distance and closing rate. Is the gap actually closing?
2. If it is not closing, stop — do not credit an erosion signal.
3. Compute cash conversion and cash coverage. Is profit arriving as money?
4. Investigate who holds the right to the cash-generating asset.
5. Only then ask whether a shock is plausible, and report it as outside the framework.
