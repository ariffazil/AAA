# Reading Instruments by Class — Commodity vs Company

Governs the recurring question "what does this chart actually tell me" when the subject is a
*company* rather than a commodity, and the follow-on "the chart was blind, so add more
layers" — which is the wrong remedy.

## The split

| | Commodity (gold, oil) | Listed company |
|---|---|---|
| Counterparty | none | CEO, board, auditor, lenders |
| Hidden state to falsify | none | accounts, related-party dealings, off-book vehicles |
| Price is | the primary data | what people currently *believe* |
| Technicals read | the instrument | the crowd's belief, one step removed |
| Can be faked | no — nothing to fake | yes, for years |

The same tool is honest on one and blind on the other, because one of them has something
hidden under the price. Never generalise a chart verdict across the split.

## Company: the chart reads the EFFECT, never the cause

The observable footprint of informed exit is a **distribution sequence**:

> support breaks → price tries back up → fails at the former support (now resistance) →
> breaks again → lower high → lower low

Mechanism: everyone who bought at the old support is trapped there. When price returns to that
level they sell to get out flat, not to profit — so fresh supply appears at every bounce.
Retail buying causes the bounce; informed holders cause the failure.

- It shows **insiders leaving**, which is a question, not an answer: *why are they leaving?*
- It cannot distinguish fraud from an ordinary decline (sector damage, margin compression, a
  refinancing wall). It does not need to — if the pattern is there, exiting first is the correct
  asymmetry. Being early out of a sound company costs opportunity; staying in a dead one costs
  everything.
- A clean chart is **not** evidence of health. "It hasn't crashed yet" fails as a test because
  the collapses are cliff-edged: information arrives at once, after the chart has been at its
  most attractive. Major accounting failures looked healthiest immediately before disclosure.
- The load-bearing question for a company is not in the chart at all — it is *who audits this,
  and are they independent of the party that pays them?* A signature from a party that profits
  from the numbers is worth nothing, however attractive the chart.

## The remedy for a blind reading is OUTSIDE the price

**Self-check before recommending anything: if you just argued the instrument cannot see the
thing, do not answer by adding indicator layers to that instrument.** More layers make you trade
less, not know more — it reads as discipline while hiding that no new information exists.

The three things that genuinely raise signal quality:

1. **A macro gate** — variables outside price: policy path, real yields, the dollar, supply
   risk. Legitimate precisely because they are not derived from the chart.
2. **A risk gate** — minimum reward:risk and position size (see `position-sizing-and-ruin.md`).
3. **An explicit unknowns section** — state what the read does not cover. A signal that shows a
   verdict with no stated blind spot is overclaiming; the honest shape is "here is the price and
   what it did / here is what moves it from outside / here is what we cannot know".

## Match the read to the question being asked

The same asset can be a trade or a valuation, and the question decides which read is valid — not
the asset's class:

- **Entry/exit over hours to days** → the chart is the whole input. Fundamentals are noise at
  that horizon; ignoring them is not laziness.
- **Hold or accumulate over years** → the accounts are the first input. The chart answers a
  different question ("where did price go") than the one asked ("what is this building").

When two people disagree about whether fundamentals matter, check first whether they are
answering the same question. They usually are not, and neither is wrong.

## Untradeable entities have no chart at all

For a wholly state-owned or unlisted entity there is no ticker, no price, and therefore no
distribution sequence — the entire early-warning mechanism above is absent. The only readout
left is the **flow**: cash leaving to the owner versus capital returning into the business. A
widening gap over successive years is that entity's chart, and only insiders can see it.
