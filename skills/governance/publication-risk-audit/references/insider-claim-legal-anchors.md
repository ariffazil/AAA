# Malaysian Publication-Risk Legal Anchors

Starting points for a solicitor, not settled positions. Law is jurisdiction-specific and moves.
Each entry states the **operative rule** the audit depends on.

---

## 1. Defamation — comment vs fact

**Fair comment requires four elements** (from *Joshua Benjamin Jeyaretnam v Goh Chok Tong*, applied
domestically):

1. the words are **comment** (may include inferences of fact);
2. the comment is on a **matter of public interest**;
3. the comment is **based on facts**; and
4. it is a comment a **fair-minded person could honestly make** on the facts proved.

**Operative rule for the audit:** the defence turns on the *form* of the sentence, not the strength
of the belief. A sentence asserting a fact carries a different burden from a sentence stating an
inference. This is why converting asserted intent into explicit first-person opinion is the highest-
leverage remediation.

**Sufficient substratum of facts.** The comment must rest on a body of facts that are **truly
stated**. The full set need not be recited, but the facts relied on must be true and must actually
exist. A writer may not invent facts and then comment on them.

**Malice defeats fair comment.** A comment made maliciously is not fair comment. Honest belief plus a
public-interest purpose is the posture that survives; grievance and score-settling is the posture
that does not.

**Precedent for the winning shape (NFCorp v Rafizi, apex court).** A statement expressed as opinion
and inference drawn from documents was held to be comment, not fact — and the defence succeeded. Note
what carried it: the underlying facts were **not disputed**, and there was an express finding of **no
malice**. The audit lesson is direct — the defence lives in the *framing*, and it is destroyed by a
single sentence that states an inference as a fact.

## 2. Defamation — what forfeits the reporting defences

**Reportage and responsible journalism are separate, mutually exclusive defences — and the publisher
must choose** (*Mkini Dotcom v Raub Australian Gold Mining*, apex court). You cannot plead both: one
says you believe the allegation is true, the other says you do not.

**Operative rules for the audit:**

- **Adoption forfeits reportage.** A publisher that espouses, concurs with, or makes the allegation
  its own loses the neutral-reporting protection. Concurrence need not be express — a headline, a
  framing sentence, or a repeated assertion can imply it.
- **The verification burden stays with the publisher.** A subject's refusal to comment does **not**
  give a free pass to publish defamatory material.
- **A source list is not verification.** The court asks what steps were taken to verify truth and
  accuracy. Labelling a figure as an estimate, or listing a secondary source, answers a different
  question. This is the single most important anchor for the audit: **grade labels document a gap,
  they do not close it.**
- **Comment carries more protection than reportage.** Reportage protects only *neutral reporting*
  and is lost the moment the author agrees with what is reported. Attack-commentary — which is what
  adversarial civic writing is — cannot shelter under reportage at all.

## 3. Employment — the social-media dismissal line

The controlling question in Malaysian industrial practice is **not** "was the post true" but
**"was the conduct inconsistent with the duty of fidelity"** — and it is decided on the employer's
policy and the facts, not on whether a defamation suit was ever filed.

**Operative rules for the audit:**

- **The employer need not sue.** Established position that an employer can dismiss for a defamatory
  post without first bringing a defamation action. So "they won't sue" is not a defence — the
  cheaper tool is a show-cause letter, not a writ.
- **Policy clauses extend beyond working hours.** Where a policy says confidentiality and loyalty
  obligations apply "beyond work hours and outside the workplace", the out-of-hours / own-device
  defence fails.
- **No need to name the employer.** Courts have held the reach of social media is such that third
  parties may be taken to know who was meant.
- **Disclosing an internal document is grave misconduct** even where the employee is otherwise long-
  serving, remorseful, and it is a first offence.
- **The line that actually decides cases is confidentiality, not tone.** Where the post disclosed no
  non-public information, caused no *proven* reputational or financial harm, and showed no malice,
  dismissal has been set aside — with compensation reduced for intemperate language. Where internal
  documents or specific non-public detail were published, dismissal was upheld.

**Therefore:** an employee-commentator's real boundary is not "be polite" or "don't be rude about the
boss". It is **"never publish what only an insider could know."** Route the audit to that line.

## 4. Criminal confidentiality exposure

Where the material concerns an employer's operational or financial information obtained in the
course of duty, the provision that tends to be used is **s.203A of the Penal Code** (disclosure of
information obtained in the course of duty), frequently read with the attempt provision (s.511). It
carries a fine and imprisonment. There is also the **Official Secrets Act 1972**, which is broader
and less predictable in scope.

**Operative rules for the audit:**

- The trigger is **not** "is the document marked confidential" — it is **"was the information
  obtained by virtue of employment."** This is why a figure that is *true and correct* can still be
  the most dangerous thing on the page: correctness is not a defence to unauthorised disclosure.
- The doctrine of avoiding harm to the employer's negotiating position is routinely advanced as the
  harm element.
- Practical detection note: internal-email forensics at the employer can establish the send and the
  recipient independently of any public reporting. Assume the metadata exists.

## 5. The audit's operating conclusion

Rank every claim by **"could an outsider have written this sentence?"**

- **Yes, with a public source** → safe. Cite it inline.
- **Yes, as inference** → safe, if expressed as inference.
- **No — only an insider could know this** → this is the finding, regardless of whether it is true,
  and regardless of how favourable the disclosure is to the public interest.

The public-interest value of the disclosure is a real moral consideration and an unreliable legal
one. State that tension plainly; do not resolve it for the principal.
