# Ownership & Control Analysis — consolidation, stake-sale and rumour questions

Companion to the page-forensics workflow. Use when the question is *who controls this institution, what constrains them, and is a transaction actually happening* — rather than *who sits on the board*.

## 1. Fix the ownership chain before answering anything else

Draw the chain as layers with a holding percentage at each step, and compute the **effective economic interest at the bottom** (multiply the chain). State it. Control at the top is not ownership at the bottom, and the gap between the two is usually where the analysis error lives.

| Layer | Holding | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Principal → listed holdco | x% | name the private vehicle, not just the individual |
| Holdco → operating entity | y% | date and price of acquisition |
| Other shareholders | z% | state fund / government / foreign strategic — and whether they want out |
| **Effective interest at the bottom** | x×y | the number most commentary never computes |

**Correct the premise in the first line if the request carries a counting error.** Requests arrive with framing baked in ("his banks", "the merger", "the deal"). Pull the licence/entity record and check the count, the plurality, and the tense. A brief that inherits a false premise has validated the rumour rather than analysed it — and the correction is frequently the single most valuable output. State it once, up front, in a clause; do not build a section on the error you are correcting.

## 2. Separate the standing rule from the specific condition

Governance constraints on ownership come in two kinds and they behave completely differently:

- **Statutory / standing** — applies to everyone, changes only by legislation. E.g. individual shareholding caps, grandfathering provisions, licensing thresholds.
- **Condition of a specific approval** — applies to one party, was granted at one moment in time, and can be extended, waived or renegotiated politically.

Say which one you are looking at. Most commentary collapses them, then treats an extendable condition as an immovable law and misprices the probability of anything happening. Conversely, do not treat a reported condition as a documented one — if the instrument itself cannot be located, mark it as reported-not-verified and say so.

## 3. Test the deadlock, don't assert it

When a transaction has repeatedly failed, the failure has a mechanism. Build the **shareholder motive matrix**: for each party, what they need, what they will not accept, and the consequence. A deadlock is only credible if at least two parties' red lines are mutually exclusive.

The recurring pair is **price and control**. Look for the tell: a seller whose stake is worth more than their own market capitalisation, or a buyer whose guidance was just cut. Both harden positions in opposite directions.

## 4. Build the base-rate ledger

When the event has been attempted before, tabulate every prior attempt: year, parties, what killed it, outcome. Ten aborted rows is a base rate, and a base rate is falsifiable in a way narrative commentary is not. **Count the completions explicitly.** If the only completed deal in the lineage involved a different seller or a different asset, say that too — it is the honest denominator.

This ledger is the credibility anchor of the whole document. Iterate it before writing prose.

## 5. Check whether the rumour has any live signal

Ask directly, and name the categories: announcement, regulator approval to negotiate, adviser mandate, capital instrument, dated negotiation report. Absence of all of them is a finding, not a gap — state what you swept (which lanes, what recency window) so the negative claim is auditable.

Then give the **monitoring tripwire list**: the named filings that would move the probabilities, in the order they would appear, plus a **kill criterion** — the evidence that retires the thesis. A rumour brief has a short shelf life; the tripwires let it expire loudly instead of silently.

## 6. Rank the under-discussed scenario

Chatter over-weights the headline outcome. Enumerate 5-7 scenarios with probabilities, and check whether the most *structurally compatible* path — usually the least dramatic one, e.g. a partial sell-down or a minority stake that satisfies the constraint while preserving control — ranks above the headline transaction. If it does, say so plainly; it is often the most useful line in the document.

## 7. Mandatory counter-narrative

Include a section making the strongest case *for* the event happening, with each point assessed. A brief that only argues one direction is advocacy, not analysis. Then close with an explicit "what this document does not know" table — gaps, and the impact of each on the conclusion.

## Output guardrails

- **No rating, price target, buy/hold/sell, or investment recommendation** on any listed entity, ever. Governance analysis describes structure; it does not advise on securities.
- **Effective interest and control are different claims.** Do not let a control conclusion stand in for an ownership one.
- **Pattern of outcomes ≠ motive.** Describe what repeatedly happened; do not attribute intent without evidence (see F6 MARUAH rule in the parent skill).
- Tag every claim as observed / derived / interpreted / speculated / absent, and keep the tags visible in the deliverable.

## Proven shape

Eighteen-year Malaysian banking consolidation question: premise correction (one licensed bank, not several) → ownership chain with effective interest calculated → regulator condition separated from statute → motive matrix → ten-row base-rate ledger counting completions → absence sweep over five signal categories → six scenarios with the under-discussed partial-sell-down ranked above the headline merger → counter-narrative → tripwire list with kill criterion.
