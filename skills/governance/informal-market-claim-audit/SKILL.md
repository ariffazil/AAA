# Informal Market Claim Audit

Confident tables are not evidence. For markets with no official statistics —
sex work, body commodification, grey-market service, side-hustle economies —
the artifact's formatting carries the confidence the data cannot supply.
Audit the claims, then keep only the mechanism that survives.

## 1. Count the markets before accepting a label

One label usually hides several distinct payment flows with different
suppliers, prices and buyer demographics. Split them by **who pays for what**:

| Flow | Buyer pays for | Body in the exchange |
|---|---|---|
| Worship / display | access to touch, pose, flex | destination |
| Role inversion (sub/service) | the strong party's submission | instrument |
| Transaction / media | posing, companionship, custom content | product |

Each flow has its own price, its own buyer age band, its own legal exposure.
An artifact that stacks them under one word produces a model nothing can
falsify. Name the split before evaluating any number.

## 2. Re-price every cost and wage line to a live source

Undated shelf prices are the most common way a sound argument dies. Check the
load-bearing line against a primary source (price-watch databases, job boards,
organiser-published prize structures) before using it to prove a deficit. A
2–3× error in one line can invert the conclusion — and a cost line labelled
"protein" that is really "total food" is exactly that error.

## 3. Separate mechanism from motive

Any claim that people do X because of a psychological need (release from
responsibility, cognitive offloading, status compensation) must name what
would refute it. If nothing could, label it HYPOTHESIS with the falsifier
missing — do not promote it to mechanism because it uses psychological
vocabulary. Prefer a measurable driver over an intuitive one even when the
intuitive one flatters the analysis more.

## 4. Verify what the citation argues, not that it exists

A real source attached to the wrong claim is worse than no source, because it
survives a link check. Read the cited passage for WHAT it supports.
Interviews about posing-for-pay do not support claims about submission-service
markets; adult-industry numbers do not transfer to private arrangements.

## 5. Ask whether the driver is capability or criminalisation

When an artifact says "dark web", "encrypted", "faceless" or "underground",
test which force builds that shape. If the acts are illegal, the channel is a
legal shield and the market's architecture follows from the statute — the
legal cover (gym, massage, coaching, personal training) is the product, not
the marketing. Misreading legal deterrence as technological novelty produces
a model that predicts the wrong thing about who enters.

## 6. Risk-adjust the yield; do not gross it

High per-session price against catastrophic downside is a negative return for
anyone with a name, career or family to lose. Such a market therefore
**selects for people who have already written off the mainstream path**, not
for elite performers who merely lost on stage. Test which way entry runs:
does participation follow competitive failure, or precede it? State the
direction you find rather than assuming the flattering one.

## 7. Digital scales deflate; only scarcity holds price

Asynchronous content — clips, photos, faceless media — has near-infinite
supply and free substitutes, so price falls toward marginal cost. Only the
physically scarce (one body, one room, one night) keeps pricing power. Treat
any "low effort, high yield digital" line as a race to the bottom until
proven otherwise.

## 8. Finish with the surviving mechanism, not the tally

The deliverable is: which one mechanism survives the audit, and which single
number the whole argument rests on. If that number is unmeasured, write
UNMEASURED and say who is carrying the load. Never invent it to complete the
model. A correct mechanism with one honest hole beats a complete model with
one fabricated input.
