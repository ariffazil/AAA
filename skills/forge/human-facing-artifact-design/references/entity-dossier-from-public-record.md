# Entity dossiers built from the public record

Method for a dossier on an organisation — an operator's acreage position, a competitor's footprint, an
institution's presence in a country. Applies with §5 of SKILL.md (probe before quoting any number); the
rules here are the entity-level counterparts to that section's number-level ones.

---

## 1. Build the position from primary releases, never from coverage of them

For any award, farm-in, PSC change or divestment, fetch the company's own release before reading the
coverage. The release names the field list, the participating interests and the block geometry; wire and
newspaper reports systematically drop all three, because none of them matter to a general reader and all
of them matter to a specialist one.

- Extract the entity names exactly, including full subsidiary names and any "collectively known as" label.
- Extract the field/asset list verbatim, and diff it against any older map or registry you were about to
  reuse. A superseded field list carried onto a map handed to someone working the acreage is the failure
  mode this rule exists to prevent.
- Tag anything that appears only in news coverage as **secondary**, and say so in the artifact.
- Check the regulator's own material too, and check its date: pages predating a restructure keep
  describing the old structure.

## 2. Split interest by field, not by asset

A single block routinely carries one percentage for one field and a different one for the rest — the
headline number is the block figure and is wrong for the field somebody is actually modelling. Where the
operator's release distinguishes them, present the table per field. Give the range and say what spans it,
rather than quoting a single number that will be right for nobody.

## 3. Include the decision-makers and what their backgrounds imply

A corporate dossier without the seats is a map without a driver. Record the seat, the incumbent, their
professional background, and the implication — marked as interpretation, never as a stated strategy.

For a state-owned operator, the pairing matters more than either half: a commercially-trained chief
executive over a geoscience-trained subsurface head reads as portfolio arithmetic at the top and
technical advocacy underneath; the reverse reads as a technical champion with commercial discipline
below. It also predicts pace — whether a committed exploration well is a technical exercise or a
compliance line item.

Note the difference between **acting** and **confirmed** in a seat. An acting title the company has not
yet ratified is a live uncertainty, and it usually coincides with the period in which the committed
wells have to be delivered.

## 4. Check whether the subject authored the primary literature on its own ground

A peer-reviewed paper whose corresponding author sits at the operator is two things at once: a capability
signal (they can run and publish subsurface work) and an authority vector — everyone who later cites the
regional framework is citing them. Resolve authorship from registered DOI metadata rather than from a
retrieval page, read the affiliation off the artifact's face, and say in the caption that it is the
operator's own account rather than independent scholarship.

## 5. Lay public affairs out in layers, and name the layer a statement belongs to

For a state-owned or state-linked entity, "is this company PR-heavy?" is the wrong question — the
question is which channel it is buying. Four layers, in descending visibility:

| Layer | Surface | What it is doing |
|---|---|---|
| National narrative | anniversary campaigns, anthems, "energy for the nation" framing, national-media placements | makes the domestic public feel ownership of the resource, which suppresses the question of price and pace |
| Technical authority | research centres, publications, conference committee seats, endowment | buys the right to define the regional framework everyone else works in |
| Ground-level licence | state-level CSR, local EPC awards to state-owned companies, ambassador and minister events, small education grants | buys access and renewal in the host jurisdiction, cheaply and photographically |
| Shadow | controversies the narrative does not address | the reason the other three are loud |

Compare against a peer national oil company on the same four axes. A company whose visibility runs
through the chief executive as a personal brand and one whose visibility runs through institutions and
community spend are different machines: the personal-brand model projects further while the executive is
strong and is fragile when they leave; the institutional model survives any single departure and speaks
more quietly the whole time.

## 6. Verify names before attaching anything to them

Two entities that share a name — or two members of one group — are the standard trap. Before attaching a
fact, a web presence or a portfolio to the subject, check the domain's own contents and the entity's own
disclosed holdings against each other. A dossier that hands a sister company's or a peer's presence to
the subject is spotted immediately by anyone inside the industry, and it discredits the parts that are
correct.

Same discipline on the individual level: full spelling, including double letters, verified against the
entity's own release rather than a news rendering of it.

## 7. Close with a named-gap register

Where the reader will want a fact no reachable source holds — a licence award date, terms, a resource
number, a headcount, a spud or first-gas date — write it as an explicit *not found / not published* row
with one line on why it matters. Do not omit it and do not estimate it. An estimate in a table of facts
contaminates the facts beside it.

Keep the two states distinct: **no public evidence found** is not **does not exist**. Give the first a
confidence and a note that absence of public evidence is not proof of absence.
