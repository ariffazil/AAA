# Compensation Claim Grading

Load when asked what a role pays, whether an offer is fair, what band to negotiate to, or whether a
figure someone quoted is real. Applies to any occupation, any market.

## The core hazard

**Public pay aggregators average across job families that share a job title and nothing else.** A site
returning an "average <occupation> salary" is usually pooling the specialist industrial population with
adjacent and junior ones — for a geoscientist, that means mine and quarry geologists, GIS technicians,
site and laboratory staff averaged together with petroleum specialists — and the result lands a factor of
two to five below the specialist market. The number is not a lie about its own population; it is a lie
about yours.

So: **never quote an aggregate without naming the population it averaged.** If the source does not
state its sample, treat it as unusable for a negotiation and say why.

## Procedure

1. **Establish which market the question is actually about** before searching. Public sector, mining,
   environmental consulting, oil and gas upstream, and tech all use the same job title at very different
   pay levels. Asking "what does a geologist earn" without this is the mistake that produces a useless
   answer.
2. **Prefer an industry-specific salary guide over a general aggregator.** A guide published for the
   sector, banded by years of experience *and* by employer tier, is worth more than any aggregated
average. Guides banding by experience alone still hide the tier effect.
3. **Check the source hierarchy, explicitly:**
   - **Strongest** — first-party: an offer letter, a payslip, a contract, a named person's account.
   - **Strong** — a sector salary survey that states its sample and bands by experience and tier.
   - **Weak** — a general aggregator with an undisclosed sample.
   - **Ancillary** — anonymous forum and social posts. Useful as a *direction* check ("is pay in this
     sector rising or falling?") and as colour, never as the figure.
4. **Extract the total package, never the base.** In resource, energy and field-based industries the base
   is systematically the smaller half. Ask for and quote the components by name — base, bonus, offshore
   or field day rate, remote/hardship allowance, hazardous-duty allowance, housing, vehicle, provident
   and medical — and say which ones are included. A base-only comparison understates an offer and
   inflates a competing one.
5. **Apply a tier adjustment before comparing any two employers.** Employers in the same industry sit in
   distinct pay tiers — operators and supermajors at the top, major service companies below them, local
   subcontractors well below. A title comparison across tiers is not a comparison.
6. **Sanity-check the result against one first-party datapoint** if any exists — a known offer, a
   reported package, the user's own current figure. If the sourced band and the first-party datapoint
   disagree by more than a band width, say so rather than smoothing it.
7. **Report a band with its level named** — "principal/lead at operator tier, not senior" — not a single
   number. A single number invites anchoring to a figure the evidence does not support.

## Negotiation-specific conduct

- **Never put a compensation figure in a CV or a self-description.** It caps the range before the
  conversation starts, and it is the one artifact the candidate does not control the interpretation of.
- **Aim the evidence at the level, not the number.** The levers that move a candidate into the next band
  are scope (asset-wide rather than single-project), accountability (recommendation carried to a decision
  rather than analysis delivered), and domain depth. When advising on positioning, work those, not the
  figure.
- **When a package is owed an assessment, name the band and the tier and stop.** Do not compute what the
  person "should" accept, and do not comment on their personal financial obligations when they mention
  them — offer to do arithmetic on a stated amount and duration if asked, and nothing more.

## Pitfalls

- Quoting a single aggregate number from a site that does not disclose its sample. The population is the finding.
- Comparing a base salary at one employer with a total package at another. Normalise before comparing.
- Reading a job-ad range as the market rate. Posted ranges reflect what an employer hoped to pay, and skew to entry and mid bands.
- Treating an hourly or annual figure as directly comparable to a monthly one without converting and stating the basis.
- Presenting a remembered figure from an earlier session as current. Probe in the session you are answering in, and state the source and its date.
- Letting an anonymous forum post carry a negotiating position. Use it to sense direction, then find a citable source for the number.
- Stating an employer's tier as fact when it is an inference from pay data. Mark the inference.
