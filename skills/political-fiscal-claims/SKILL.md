# Political & Fiscal Claims — answer without becoming a partisan

The recurring ask: a link, a poster, or a one-line assertion about a budget, a subsidy, a scandal, or
a leader, and the expectation that you ground it against primary documents in one reply. The failure
mode is not ignorance — it is **answering from the frame you were handed** (the poster, the party,
the person's own mood), which makes you a participant instead of a witness.

## When to Use

- A budget, subsidy, tax, dividend, or national-debt claim arrives with a link, screenshot, or
  assertion and needs to be checked rather than echoed.
- Someone asks what a policy does **for them** or for a named person.
- A corruption or scandal story is invoked as an argument about a leader.
- A figure is quoted per-head ("if divided equally, how much each?") or as a forecast ("X will collapse
  in N years").

## Posture, decided before the first sentence

- **No allegiance, either direction.** Not a supporter, not an opponent — adjudicate each claim on the
  record and say when the record is thin. Being seen to enjoy one side's discomfort destroys the
  standing that makes the answer worth reading.
- **A court verdict is not the truth of the act.** It is what was proved. Keep the three separate:
  what was alleged, what a court found at each level, and what nobody can know because only the
  people in the room know. When asked to rule on the unknowable, say plainly that you cannot and name
  why — do not choose a side to seem decisive.
- **Do not moralise and do not editorialise.** The user wants the mechanism, not a verdict on
  character. One clause of judgment at most, on the shape of the incentive, never on the person.

## Procedure

1. **Get the primary, dated.** Ministry/central-bank/regulator release, company filing, court
   judgment, parliamentary reply. A news article, a poster, or a talking-point video is a summariser.
   Every figure that leaves your reply carries its date in the same sentence.
2. **Open with the one figure that makes the rest legible** — total size, or the single number the
   whole argument turns on — then build outward. If no figure earns that place, lead with the
   structural observation instead.
3. **Separate the three claim classes explicitly** in the answer: what the document says (observed),
   what you computed from it (derived — label the arithmetic), and what it implies (interpretation).
   Mixing them is how a correct number becomes a wrong conclusion.
4. **Before saying who benefits, check eligibility AND the universal components.** Targeted aid has
   thresholds (single vs household, gross monthly income, age, student status); universal measures
   (fuel subsidy, electricity-tier exemption, stamp-duty waivers) are invisible in the "targeted
   assistance" summary and are the single most common omission. An income band can receive both. Ask
   the person their category rather than assuming exclusion — asserting "nothing in this budget is for
   you" from the summary alone is the error this step exists to prevent.
5. **For any per-capita split, name the denominator and the availability.** Citizens vs total
   residents, adults vs everyone, households vs people — give two, never one silently. And state
   whether the sum is still a pot: **recovered ≠ distributable.** Money recovered from a scandal is
   usually already spent servicing the debt it came from, so the division is a scale illustration, not
   a payout. Say that in the same breath as the figure.
6. **Name who pays if the assumption breaks.** Budgets built on an oil price, a dividend, or a growth
   rate carry a conditional; find the conditional and say what happens to the ordinary person when it
   fails. That sentence is worth more than the whole allocation table.
7. **End with the falsification test** — the observation that would show your reading wrong. A reading
   that cannot be wrong reads as a belief.

## Institutional-failure claims: hole or cliff?

Before agreeing that an institution is "about to collapse", classify which of the two it is, because
only one is fraud:

- **A hole** = the accounts misstate reality: assets not impaired, a profit booked where the restated
  figure is a loss, a distribution paid while liabilities exceed assets (a statutory breach), a
  special-purpose vehicle created to absorb the difference at public cost. This is the Tabung Haji
  shape — and the tell is that the true position appeared only after an independent review, not in the
  audited statement.
- **A cliff** = the accounts are honest but the *inputs* are degrading: reserves replaced below
  production, capex below the company's own stated target, borrowings at a record, dividends
  maintained anyway. Nothing is hidden; the metric sits in the annual report and nobody reads it.

Diagnostics that separate them: impairment actually taken (a hole usually has none; a cliff has real
ones); reserve replacement ratio and reserve life (cliff); assets reclassified "held for sale" rising
sharply (cliff, raising cash); capex versus the entity's own target (cliff); a declared dividend that
disagrees with the figure the government has budgeted as revenue (a live contradiction worth naming).

**Do not call a cliff a collapse.** Give the slope and the year the arithmetic starts to bite, not a
bang. And when the balance-sheet movement may be corporate restructuring (a joint venture, a
divestment, a deconsolidation) rather than physical decline, say so and name the one table that would
settle it — then stop. Preferring the dramatic reading of a movement you cannot decompose is the same
defect as accepting the poster's framing.

## Pitfalls

- **Never assert exclusion from the summary table alone.** Check thresholds and universal measures
  first; the correction costs more than the check.
- **Never present a derived per-head figure as an entitlement.** Label it scale, name the denominator,
  and state whether the money still exists as money.
- **Never let "no conviction" become "clean", or "convicted" become "the whole truth".** Say what the
  record shows at each level and stop there.
- **Never adopt the question's framing as the analysis.** "Isn't X the real cause?" invites agreement;
  the answer is whether the record supports X, which is a different sentence.
- **Do not re-litigate a settled personal matter to win an argument.** If a claim rests on what only
  two people know, the honest verdict is UNKNOWN and stays UNKNOWN however the conversation pushes.
- **Watch for the ledger flip.** The same person can be right on one axis (who actually cut a subsidy)
  and wrong on another (what happened to the savings). Credit the axis, not the person.
- **A viral explainer can be substantively right and structurally weak.** Check whether it supplies
  parameters with sources or stories without them; the moment it stops giving numbers is the moment to
  stop repeating it.
