# Body Rehab Advisory — post-op and injury timelines

Depth for the case where a person asks about their own recovery from an operation or injury
(orthopaedic, post-surgical, return-to-sport). Read alongside the SKILL.md procedure, not instead of it.

## Establish four facts before advising anything

1. **What was the operation, and what was repaired versus removed.** A repaired structure (a
   sutured structure, a reconstructed ligament) gates far harder and for far longer than a debrided
   or trimmed one. Ask what *else* was done in the same operation — the limiting tissue is often not
   the one in the procedure's name.
2. **Donor or graft source, where relevant.** An autograft harvest site is its own pain generator for
   months, and it changes which exercises are restricted early.
3. **Current measured range of motion — and whether the block is pain or mechanical.** Ask the person
   to self-measure rather than theorising: prone heel-to-buttock with a strap or towel gives a flexion
   number with no equipment. Whether they stop because it hurts or because it physically will not go
   further splits the case into two completely different problems.
4. **Location of the pain, precisely.** Where it hurts attributes which structure the movement loads.
   Anterior patellar or notch pain, posterior joint-line pain, and calf tightness have different
   mechanisms and different remedies.

## Separate the two deficits

Range and load tolerance are independent. "Can the joint get there?" and "can the tissue take body
weight in that position?" have separate ceilings, separate exercises, and separate timelines. A person
can report near-full range and still be unable to load the joint — do not collapse the second problem
into the first.

Time since surgery is a weak signal. Criteria are the gate: which tests the person passes, and how
the operated side compares with the other side. Do not anchor advice to a week number.

## The progression ladder

Give a self-regulating ladder rather than a target: softest surface first (mattress, then thick pillow,
then floor), short holds, high frequency, advance **one variable at a time**, stop on sharp pain or on
next-day swelling. Padding under the shin or ankle lets the person approximate a position without
loading it fully — useful when a repair in that region makes full compression unsafe.

## Structural facts worth holding

- **Deep flexion under body weight is the highest-load position** for posterior joint-line tissue and
  for the patellofemoral joint. If a repair sits in that region, deep kneeling is the last thing to
  return — it is a loading problem, not a flexibility problem to stretch through.
- **A stiff ankle or calf silently caps kneeling.** Sitting on the heels is impossible with a stiff
  ankle even at full knee range. Always screen it before blaming the knee.
- **Harvest-site pain is not surgical failure.** Patellar-tendon graft sites are notoriously sore under
  direct pressure and can stay that way for a long time; hamstring grafts are usually kinder to kneel on.
- **Strength asymmetry, not range, governs return to sport**, and the harvest side is typically the
  slowest to recover. Defer to limb-symmetry strength measures and hop tests; the treating clinician
  owns the clearance threshold.
- **A reconstruction can be too tight.** Persistent mechanical blocking, pinching at the front of the
  joint, or a loss of full extension is a clinician conversation, not a stretching project.

## Religious practice under a movement restriction

When the restricted movement is part of worship, say plainly that a licensed alternative exists (pray
seated, or from a chair/stool) and that the person should confirm the ruling with their own religious
authority. Do not let someone force a repaired joint into a position to avoid taking a concession —
and do not issue the religious ruling yourself.

## Routing and framing

- Give **the single decisive question** to put to the treating clinician — the one that changes the
  plan — rather than closing with a blanket "ask your doctor". That question is the part the person
  cannot construct on their own.
- Name the red flags that end self-management and route straight to the surgeon: new swelling,
  giving way, locking, fever, calf pain with swelling, wound drainage.
- State the frame once, plainly: general rehab logic, not a prescription; the clinician owns the
  protocol. Do not hedge every sentence into mush, and do not drop the frame either.
- If a bespoke artifact already exists for this person (plan, PDF, dashboard), **update it** rather
  than re-deriving the content — and check whether a newly disclosed clinical fact invalidates its
  gating criteria. For the artifact-side rules when rebuilding it (carry the binding constraint,
  ship the clinician's unknowns as questions, ladder not verdict, what furniture is allowed), see
  `human-facing-artifact-design` §4.
