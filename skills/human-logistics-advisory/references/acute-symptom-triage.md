# Acute Symptom Triage — a person reports pain or illness in chat

Read before answering anything a person says about an acute symptom in themselves: pain, a flare,
dizziness, bleeding, something that just happened. This is the acute sibling of
`body-rehab-advisory.md`, which covers planned recovery from a known procedure.

## The one rule

You cannot examine, so you cannot diagnose. State what can be checked, hand over the ladder, and let a
clinician decide. A plausible mechanism is not a finding: naming a cause you cannot verify — "it's just
gas", "that's stress", "it'll pass" — is the failure shape, because it reassures, it closes the door,
and it is wrong a predictable fraction of the time. The same applies in reverse: never inflate a
benign picture into alarm to look careful.

## Procedure

1. **Get the ONSET before computing elapsed time.** "You've been in this three hours" is a claim and
   needs a known start. If the person never said when it began, ask — the arithmetic that feels like
   summarising is the assertion that lands wrong, and in a care exchange the timeline *is* the payload.
2. **Screen the red flags BEFORE any other question.** They are the ones that make the decision for
   you, and they end the questionnaire. If any fires, say go now and stop asking.
3. **Then ask a short set of CLOSED questions**, four to six, each answerable in a word. Someone in
   pain will not write you an essay; open questions get a paragraph of guesswork or nothing.
4. **Give the ladder, not a verdict.** Three rungs — *go now* / *watch for N and go if worse* /
   *self-care* — with what moves someone between them. The rung assignment is the person's to make
   against their own body; your job is to make each rung checkable.
5. **Name the transport, not the diagnosis.** When a companion is present and willing, the thing that
   actually changes the outcome is someone who can drive. Say plainly that you cannot assess and that
   the person who can move is the one who can act. Do not convert a willingness to help into a
   second opinion.
6. **Do not keep going after a red flag.** The point of a ladder is that one red flag ends the
   interview. Continuing to collect answers after one has fired is the agent preferring the complete
   picture to the person's outcome.

## Red flags — any one ends the questionnaire

Give the reader these as a list they own and can act on without you:

- Abdomen rigid and board-like, **with no wind passed and no stool** — a different picture from pain
  alone, and the inability to pass either is the discriminating part.
- Pain that migrates, then settles and localises, with the area hurting more on release than on press.
- Cold sweat, pallor and a racing pulse **without** fever — the body diverting blood somewhere.
- Vomiting blood, or stool that is black and tarry.
- Fever with an abdomen that is tender and guarded to touch.
- Pain arriving in waves severe enough that the person cannot stay still in any position.

## The strongest AMBER discriminator

**"I took my usual medicine and nothing changed."** A home remedy that produces no response inside its
normal working window is data, and it is the single most useful non-red-flag signal. Pair it with a
clock: *when did you take it, and is it better, the same, or worse than before?* Same or worse past its
window is the rung that moves.

## Pitfalls

- **Never explain an acute episode with the stress/gut loop.** Gut-to-brain and brain-to-gut signalling
  are both real, which means "is it stress or is it physical" has no single answer and no first cause —
  the loop exits through sleep, regular food and stopping the irritant, not through insight. That makes
  it useless *now*, and using it to explain away acute pain is dismissal with a mechanism attached.
- **"I get this all the time" is not a reason to relax the red-flag rule.** A recurrent pattern is a
  conversation for a calm moment — a referral, a diet and timing review, stopping the irritant — not
  for the acute one. Familiar pain is still capable of being the serious presentation.
- **Do not hand a monitoring list to the person in pain.** A tripwire belongs to whoever will be
  present and capable: the companion, the one who can drive. Handing the checklist to someone who is
  hurting converts them into the instrument of their own surveillance.
- **Ask about the clearance questions explicitly.** Wind passed and bowel movement are not small talk;
  they separate a benign picture from a different one. If the answer is "neither", the rung moves.
- **This is not a body directive aimed at an absent person.** When a third party is discussing someone,
  the triage goes to the person who can act on it and nothing is aimed at the one who is not in the
  room. When the person is reporting on themselves and asking, answer them directly and fully.
- **Do not editorialise the later conversation.** The follow-up (see a clinician about the pattern) is
  one clause at a calm moment, not a paragraph appended to the acute answer, and not repeated after it
  has been said once.
