# Principal Submission Authoring

A document the principal signs and hands to an institution — a career-aspiration form, an
application, a claim, a complaint, a tenancy notice, a portal submission. It is not personal prose
and not a report: it is his position, entered into a process he does not control, read later by
people who were not in the conversation that produced it.

Companion skills: rendering belongs to `forge-pdf-delivery`; sending belongs to `email-outbound` /
`telegram-direct-delivery`; his personal-document voice belongs to `human-voice-writing`; the
substantive law and tax of an employment exit to `malaysian-employment-separation`. This skill owns
the **position and the channel** — what the document may say, to whom, and in which direction.

## Trigger

- "Fill in this form", "draft my answers", "write this letter for me", "send this in for me".
- Any document that will be submitted, filed, or forwarded under his name to an employer, agency,
  landlord, university, bank, or court.
- Any request of the form "make it aligned with my reality", "make it human <name>", "make it sound
  like me" applied to something an institution will read.

## Intake — settle the position before drafting a word

Drafting before this step is the expensive failure: every unanswered item below becomes a rewrite.

1. **Who reads it, and who decides.** They are usually different people, and the decider is often not
   in the room the document lands in. Content safe for the reader can still be ammunition for the
   decider; content that moves the decider can be invisible to the reader.
2. **What he wants, needs, and does not want.** Get all three. "What do you want out of this" alone
   yields a wish; the *does-not-want* list is what stops you writing something that helps him into
   the outcome he is trying to avoid.
3. **Is there a second path?** Many submissions are one half of a two-sided position — the person is
   leaving, unless something changes. Ask what would make the opposite outcome acceptable. A document
   that registers only one side makes the institution's cheapest option the one he already named.
4. **What is already in writing.** Search for the prior emails, messages, or filings he has already
   sent the same institution. That text is both the voice sample and the baseline the new document
   must not contradict.
5. **What does the document actually commit him to.** Read the form's own wording: many registration
   forms capture intent only and the binding step is a separate formal submission later. Say which
   document he is really signing up to, and where it is still reversible.
6. **Is there a due date, and what does silence cost.** A submission deadline is a real constraint;
   so is the fact that a process which receives a document need not reply to it.

## Voice — his register, not the institution's

1. **Mine his own prior outbound text before writing.** If he has already written to the same
   recipient, that document is the register to match. A submission that reads differently from his
   own email costs him credibility even when both are true — the reader sees two positions, not two
   documents.
2. **Short sentences, one idea each, no consultant veneer.** "My priority is one thing: finish the
   handover properly" beats "My interest lies in specialist technical contribution." Test: if a
   management consultant could have written the sentence, rewrite it.
3. **Ship both languages when both are legitimate.** Institutional language for the field, his own
   language beneath or beside it, and say plainly that one is chosen and the two are not to be mixed.
   Do not silently pick for him.
4. **Keep the machinery out.** No internal doctrine, no ledger or receipt vocabulary, no ids, no
   system names, no reasoning traces in anything an institution will read.

## Records: make them two-sided when the position is

- A one-sided record is self-fulfilling. If the document says only "he is leaving", the process will
  allocate him as leaving, and the alternative never gets offered or priced.
- **State preference order, not an ultimatum.** "I would rather X; if that path is not open, my
  position is Y." A price gets answered. A threat gets managed, and managed usually means declined.
- **A form is a record; a grade, an approval, or a concession is decided by a human in a
  conversation.** Put the preference order in the document so the file is not one-sided, then make
  the actual ask to the person who holds the decision. Asking for the thing inside a form routes it
  into a process, and processes do not grant discretionary asks.
- **Do not soften a statement just because it looks like ambition.** Check which ladder the target
  sits on before warning him a line closes a door — "technical, not managerial" is the opening, not
  the closing, when the target is an individual-contributor ladder.

## Channel discipline — each ask in the one place it can land

| Ask | Channel | Why there |
|---|---|---|
| Preference order, commitment, one dated conversation request | the form / application | it becomes the official record and must not be one-sided |
| Eligibility, and *which clause* the assessment was made under | written enquiry to the counterparty's process owner | a discretionary answer cannot cite a clause; asking for one turns a refusal into a reviewable position |
| Terms, figures, definitions, dates, written statement of a computation | written enquiry | an oral exchange is reshaped when it reaches a third party |
| The discretionary ask itself — grade, exception, waiver | conversation with the decision maker | forms cannot grant what a human must decide |
| Grievance, named colleagues, feelings about a manager | nowhere, or a trusted human | it becomes the counterparty's ammunition once filed |

- **Name one target, then stop.** "At least X", "more senior", "a better position" invite an answer
  at the same resolution — vague in, vague out.
- **Make the in-document ask small, dated, and addressed.** "I would welcome a conversation" reads as
  politeness and is answered by nothing. "A 30-minute conversation with <named person> within two
  weeks" is grantable, and its expiry converts silence into information instead of an open wait.
- **Every written preference needs a trigger.** Where the default outcome of a process is silence, a
  preference statement with no date and no owner simply parks him. Attach the date; then the next move
  — written enquiry, formal submission, escalation — has a start condition.

## Content hygiene — what never goes in a document he signs

- **Anything he would not say out loud in the deciding room.** Grievances, named individuals, and
  complaints about systems are removed by default; a submission is not the channel for them.
- **Anything that looks like the arithmetic of his own exit.** Dates, counts of completed periods,
  tax treatment, and package maths stay out of forms and go into a written enquiry or a professional
  adviser's hands. A person visibly optimising a date hands the counterparty the reason to protect it.
- **Commitments he has not decided to make.** Open-ended phrases ("and beyond where required") are
  read as an unbounded undertaking. Before pasting a choice, read the option text for a commitment
  with no end date and pick the bounded alternative.
- **Any fact you cannot source.** Identity fields, dates of service, and amounts come from him, never
  from inference. If a field is missing from his material, leave a labelled blank and ask — never
  fill it plausibly.

## After submission — the receipt

1. **Tell him to capture the submitted state before he presses send**: screenshot every field, and the
   confirmation page after. The institution keeps its copy; the submitter usually keeps none.
2. **File the artifact durably** under a dated workspace path with the rendered file, the source, and
   a manifest carrying the hash, the recipient, and the message id.
3. **Record the promise the document makes** — a conversation requested within two weeks is a
   commitment with a date, and a future session needs to know when it falls due.

## Pitfalls

- **Drafting the document before extracting the position.** Three versions of the same submission in
  one sitting is the signature of a missing intake conversation, not of a hard problem. Ask the
  two-paths question first; it costs one turn and saves the rewrites.
- **Sending every revision.** While he iterates on a submission, not every version needs mailing to
  him. Send the version that carries a decision, and say what changed from the last in two lines.
- **Matching the institution's register because the form is formal.** The form's language is
  corporate boilerplate; his position in it should still read as a person. Formal is not impersonal.
- **Leaving him with a document he cannot paste.** A submission delivered only as a PDF makes him
  retype it on a phone. Put the paste-ready blocks in the message body too.
- **Treating the submission as the decision.** Most forms buy an option; the binding act is usually
  elsewhere. Say which stage he is at so he does not believe he has committed.
