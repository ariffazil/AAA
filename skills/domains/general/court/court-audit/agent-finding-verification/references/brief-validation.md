# Validating a Brief Before It Is Used or Forwarded

A lengthy, confident, well-sourced brief can still be wrong in three ways that survive a sourcing audit
entirely. Run all three before the brief is reused, forwarded, or carried into a meeting.

## 1 · Establish the source's OWN date

News and corporate pages commonly render *today's* date in a masthead, nav bar or "updated" strip while
the article body carries the real dateline further down. **A page that reads as current is not evidence
that it is.**

- Read the body for the dateline — `(Feb 28)`, `Updated:`, a byline date — before a figure from it
  enters a "this week" frame. The body wins over the rendered date.
- Two sources agreeing on a number can still be a year apart in time. Agreement and currency are
  separate properties.
- This is the highest-yield single check on any briefing: it converts a "current" reading back into
  the date the material was actually true.

## 2 · Recompute the draft's own arithmetic

Every figure in a draft can be individually sourced and the draft can still contradict itself.
Recompute — do not read.

- **Ratios and multiples.** 15→40 is 2.67×, not "three times". A rounded-up multiplier is a different
  claim, and anyone who divides catches it instantly.
- **Countdowns and intervals.** "N days left" is arithmetic against an anchor date. It drifts the moment
  the sentence is reused or the session runs long. Recompute from the date written in the text.
- **Shares, deltas and sums.** Percentages that must total 100, parts that must sum to a whole, a
  change computed from two levels, a per-unit figure derived from two prices.
- **Labels as well as values.** Spot vs futures, opening vs closing, national vs state, gross vs net,
  production vs exports, stock vs flow. A right number under a wrong label is a wrong number.

A draft whose headline figures are all correct and whose derived figures are not has almost certainly
been assembled from source quotes rather than from its own analysis. That is a useful signal in itself.

## 3 · Check what the draft says about the READER

When a brief makes a claim about the reader's own role, business unit, portfolio or history, check it
twice — once for fact, once for **placement**.

- The specific trap: the draft draws a correct general map of an industry or system, then drops the
  reader onto the wrong part of it. The map can be sound while the placement is wrong — audit the
  placement separately from the map.
- This costs more credibility than a wrong figure, because the reader can check it without looking
  anything up. A brief that misplaces someone inside their own industry reads as not knowing their job.
- Related: a recommendation that silently assumes a fact about the reader's work which the brief never
  states. Name the assumption or drop the recommendation.

## 4 · Run the thesis backward

Before accepting the brief's central claim, search *against* it — one live query shaped to refute the
core thesis, not to support it. Record the result either way.

- A thesis that has only ever been searched *for* is a preference with citations.
- If the counter-query contradicts the thesis, the thesis becomes CONTESTED rather than "mostly true".
- **A counter-query that returns nothing is a result and must be reported.** Silently dropping it is how
  confirmation bias launders itself.
- Expect the thesis to survive at a smaller size than it was stated. "Conditions are deteriorating"
  frequently collapses to "conditions are deteriorating *in this slice*", and the smaller version is the
  one worth carrying.

## 5 · Report shape

Deliver three lists, in this order:

1. **What verified** — with the probe or live source that verified it.
2. **What did not** — the correction, stated once, plainly, and early. Lead with it; never bury a
   correction behind praise for the rest of the document.
3. **What could not be checked** — the wall, named and not padded. Naming a gap reads as rigour;
   padding it reads as noise; inventing it is disqualifying.

**Retract fast when the report turns out to be right and your suspicion was wrong.** A correction made
in the same breath costs one message and preserves the rest of the read; a suspicion you carry costs the
reader's trust in every later finding you report.

## Pitfalls

- **Do not validate by agreeing.** A summary that endorses everything is worthless to the reader who
  asked for an audit. Find the defect or say plainly that you found none.
- **Do not confuse many sourced numbers with a verified document.** Ten correct figures do not make the
  eleventh correct, and none of them make the *derived* figures correct.
- **Do not accept a report's claim that it verified something.** A reported test result, tally or check
  with no artefact beside it is unrepeatable, and an unrepeatable result is a claim, not evidence.
  Search for the artefact; absence is the finding.
- **Time-sensitive figures age inside the draft.** A figure sourced correctly last month is stale this
  month. Where a figure anchors a recommendation, re-probe it rather than re-quoting it.
