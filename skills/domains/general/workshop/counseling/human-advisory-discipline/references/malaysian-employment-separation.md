# Malaysian Employment Separation — MSS / VSS Decision Support

Load when advising a Malaysian employee on a separation offer (MSS, VSS, retrenchment) or on a
career decision driven by an employer's restructuring. Applies to any Malaysian employer; the
organisation's own published restructuring facts are a separate input.

## 1. Convert the offer to months before anything else

Separation packages are usually expressed in months of salary. If quoted as cash, divide by
monthly salary. If quoted as a formula, compute it:

```
package_months = (completed_years × multiplier) + fixed_bonus_months
```

Then compute the comparators:

| Comparator | Formula |
|---|---|
| Statutory minimum | yrs × (20/30) for >5y service (10 days/yr <2y; 15 days/yr 2–5y; 20 days/yr >5y) |
| Continued employment | months of salary earned by working the same horizon |
| Real runway | net package ÷ actual monthly burn |

**The reframe that decides the conversation:** when package months ≈ continued-employment
months, the money is not a windfall — it is purchased time. Say this out loud. The decision then
becomes *what will be done with N months*, not *how much is received*. A person leaving with
N months of salary who would have earned N months of salary by staying is financially identical
either way; what differs is who owns the time.

## 2. Step-ups track the service anniversary, not the employer's milestone

Package step-ups follow completed years of service, not the employer's org-design or handover
date. Waiting past a stated milestone can add nothing, while waiting a few weeks to the next
anniversary can add a whole step. Always compute the package at three points — today, next
anniversary, and the employer's named milestone — and name the optimum decision window.

- **The anniversary date is an assumption until the instrument says otherwise.** "Completed
  years" must be counted from a reference date the employer picks — last day of service,
  acceptance date, scheme cut-off, or a fixed scheme date — and the choice can be worth a full
  step. Never state a computed step-up date as a fact. Derive it, then say which reference
  convention it rests on and that confirming that convention is a question for HR. A date you
  inferred and delivered as an answer will be tested immediately by the only person who can see
  the document, and the retraction costs more trust than the answer was worth.
- **A stated formula is not a stated reference date.** When the person gives you the package
  formula but not the counting convention, you have the multiplier and not the clock. Say so in
  the same message, mark the resulting figure as conditional, and keep any recommendation that
  turns on it provisional until the convention is confirmed.

## 3. Statutory minimum is what defines whether the deal is fair

Ask for the statutory baseline explicitly and report the *multiple* (package ÷ statutory).
Around 2.5× is a fair package. Below ~1.5× is worth negotiating. The multiple is the only
honest score of the offer; the gross figure tells the person nothing.

## 4. Tax — never assert the net, always route to an advisor

- Compensation for loss of employment is **partially exempt at RM10,000 per completed year of
  service** for cessations on/after 1 July 2008 (LHDN Public Ruling 1/2012; para 15, Sch 6,
  ITA 1967). The RM20,000 rate was a temporary 2020–2021 measure — never apply it outside
  those years of assessment.
- **The retraction trap:** a separation-scheme payment under subpara 15(3) Sch 6 is exempt only
  if the scheme does **not** expressly or impliedly provide for re-employment by the same or any
  other employer. If the employer's own questionnaire offers both "remain" and "take separation"
  options, implied re-employment becomes arguable and the entire per-year exemption can fail —
  a materially larger tax bill, not a rounding error.
- The lump sum stacks on top of the same year's salary, so it is taxed at the top marginal rate.
  Compute the marginal delta (tax with minus tax without), never a flat rate on the whole sum.
- Present the net as an estimate with the assumption stated and the mechanism named. Send the
  person to a tax advisor with the offer document; do not be the authority on their liability.

## 5. The invisible costs

- **EPF employer contribution:** ~12% of salary (13% below RM5,000). Each year not employed
  forfeits roughly 1.5 months of salary in retirement savings — before compounding. Two years
  out is not only "two years of expenses"; it is also most of a year of salary permanently
  absent from EPF. Price it.
- **Medical / insurance continuity** ends or changes on separation. Establish the continuation
  period before deciding.
- **Bonus pro-ration, notice pay, untaken-leave encashment** — confirm whether these are in
  addition to the package or absorbed into it. The stated formula is often not the whole package.
- **Check what the multiplier is applied to:** basic salary or total cash including allowances
  and site/offshore premiums. If basic only and allowances are large, the real package is far
  smaller than the headline.

## 6. Dependant horizon — the runway that does not exist

Total up the commitment the person is already locked into: mortgage remaining, youngest child's
age to tertiary entry, their own retirement age. If a far-future dependant event lands at or
after the person's own retirement age, **there is no slack year** — the package buys a bridge
that has to reach the far side within its own length. State this plainly; it is the single fact
that most often reverses a "take the money" instinct.

## 7. The two questions that actually decide it

1. **Is there a door on the other side?** A standing offer, a business already generating
   revenue covering roughly 60% of salary, or an employer already courting them. If yes —
   separation wins: the package funds a calm transition rather than a panicked one. If no — the
   cash buys time to *build* the door; it is not a holiday, and it is not an answer.
2. **Is the role inside the employer's target category?** Restructurings target named
   categories (usually administrative/support/enabling functions, not the technical core).
   Someone inside the organisation reads their own exposure far better than any public
   reporting. Ask them rather than asserting from outside.

If the answer to (1) is no, the recommendation is to stay, declare the plain-stay intent on any
questionnaire (section 9 — declare, do not hedge), and spend the interval building the door — not
to refuse the package forever.

## 8. Employer scripts to expect

- *"Register your interest in separation, if available"* — a signalled interest is visible to
  placement panels; it marks the person as a flight risk, and "if available" is not a guarantee.
- *"Remain and support the transition until <milestone> and beyond, where required"* — the
  "and beyond" is the real term. It is a commitment question, not a date question.
- **A questionnaire that demands a choice before disclosing package terms is a timing trap.**
  Answer the choice on the merits — the form is not a leverage venue. Ask for the package
  formula, eligibility criteria and indicative timeline through a human channel (line manager,
  HR, the scheme briefing), not inside the declaration text. Which channel is authoritative for
  eligibility is a question to confirm, not to assume: a questionnaire that merely signals
  interest and a formal register (portal, written application) are different instruments.
- Never advise signing before a written term sheet and a tax advisor's read.
- **An insider's "you are not eligible" is a claim to verify, not a finding to accept — and it can dissolve the whole question.** A line manager, a peer or a senior may volunteer that the person is too junior, too new, or otherwise outside the scheme. Check the criterion against the instrument and HR before building any strategy on the exit path, because the two outcomes are entirely different problems: if a real gate exists, the exit question closes and the only live question becomes what the person builds over the next several years *inside* the organisation; if no gate exists, the exit question stays open and the earlier work stands. Do not let a verbal eligibility assertion — in either direction — become the premise of the analysis.
- **Recovering from an unguarded verbal declaration.** A person under prolonged uncertainty may declare an exit intent out loud, under stress, to the person who holds their placement card — before any decision was made. Triage it before the person acts further: separate the *spoken* channel from the *written* one (a comment in a corridor is not a filed record, and the formal route for scheme interest is usually a separate system); then **do not add a written contradiction** to a form read by that same decision-maker, because a document that disagrees with what they heard reads as not listening. The recovery line is a neutral restatement that neither repeats nor retracts the declaration ("I was thinking aloud about options; my priority is the handover"), and the topic is not raised again by the adviser's suggestion. The deeper read to name: a declaration made to relieve uncertainty is not a decision, and the scheme is not the container for that anxiety.

## 9. Register and the record — how the instrument is read

### Answering the form

- In Malay corporate and GLC convention, a restructuring questionnaire about career intent is a
  **record of intent**: read by superiors and HR, filed, quoted back later. State the intent
  plainly, in one register, with no conditional hedges. "I intend to stay, but only if…" is read
  as absent intent, and that costs more than any option the hedge preserved.
- A form deadline is a **submission** date, not an exit date. Never write a departure date, a
  notice period, or anything else that fixes a last day into the form; keep the real last day
  past the next service anniversary (section 2).
- The declaration must agree with what has already been said out loud. If interest in a scheme
  has already been stated verbally, the form carries it; if it has never been stated, the form
  carries intent only and the scheme interest goes through its own formal route. Two channels
  telling two different stories is the failure mode.
- Fill the whole instrument, not just the required fields — management reads all of it — and tick
  the "email me a receipt of my responses" box. A dated record of what was submitted removes a
  later argument about what was said.
- Before composing the answer, read the flow: what the questions are actually measuring (as intent,
  as placement interest, as development planning) and whether the person has already declared the
  same position through another channel.

### Reading the institution's published record

For a restructuring decision at a state-owned, GLC or listed employer, the public record is
admissible evidence and the person usually expects it to be read. Pull it and quote it with dates
and figures: latest full-year profit and revenue direction, dividend paid to the owner versus the
prior year, announced hiring and promotion freezes, leadership contract extensions, restructuring
windows already closed, and any litigation that threatens the business itself.

- **Separate the framing from the accounting, and prefer the institution's own audited layer.**
  A survival narrative published alongside a multi-billion profit is a communication choice, not
  a financial fact. Report both, name the framing as framing, and let the person weigh it. Where
  the employer publishes both a results announcement and a fuller financial report, the sharpest
  form of the critique quotes the audited document against the announcement: a disproof sourced
  from the institution's own filing cannot be dismissed as an outsider's opinion, which is what
  makes it the version that survives being shown to the employer.
- **Quote the leadership's own words, then stop.** Do not praise or attack the person's boss; they
  work there and the advice has to survive Monday morning.
- **Surface what the instrument omits.** A questionnaire that asks about intent while saying
  nothing about the structural threat to the business, or about the terms of the scheme it
  dangles, is itself a finding worth stating.
- What the person already knows outranks what the search returns — they read their own
  organisation better than any public source. Use the record to fill gaps and to hand them numbers
  for a conversation they are already having; never to tell them their own company.

### Adjudicating a "the employer will fail" thesis

A principal who fears collapse arrives with a chain of premises. Four moves settle it:

- **Separate solvency from trajectory.** "Will it go bust?" and "is it contracting?" are different
  questions with different answers, and an entity can be comfortably solvent while shrinking fast.
  Refuse the false binary: report the level (cash, profit, gearing) *and* the rate (change across
  the last two reporting periods). A balance sheet that lost a large share of its net cash over
  six months is the real finding, and it is invisible in the level. The decision is usually driven
  by the rate.
- **Test each premise; do not debate the conclusion.** A thesis assembled from several dated
  claims is settled by checking the premises against sources. One premise is often simply wrong —
  a leader reported as departing who has just had a contract extended, a milestone that has
  already passed, a deadline that has moved. Correcting that single premise does more than any
  counter-argument, and it is also how you keep the person's trust: you are repairing their
  evidence, not contradicting their judgement. State which premises verified, which did not, and
  which the record cannot settle.
- **Find the variable the paperwork omits.** Where an employer's ability to keep employing
  someone depends on an external matter — litigation, a licence, a state or political dispute, a
  settlement — and the internal instrument is silent on it, that variable governs the decision and
  the instrument does not. Name it as the first-order question and the personal terms as
  second-order. Surfacing it is often the single most useful thing in the whole reply.
- **Date the instrument, not the collapse.** A separation scheme is a one-shot instrument: it
  exists *because* a transition is under way, and it is funded to move people out in an orderly
  way before a new structure locks. So the useful question is not "when will this employer
  fail" — an entity can stagnate for a decade while paying everyone — but "when does this option
  close, and what is it worth after that?" Restating the timing question this way converts a
  forecast nobody can make into a window somebody can observe. It also gives the person a reason
  to act that does not depend on the collapse thesis being true, and it is the one version that
  survives the employer recovering: a solvent employer that has finished restructuring has no
  reason to pay a young specialist to leave.

## 10. Settling a small mortgage first

If outstanding housing debt is small relative to the package (roughly ≤10%), clearing it is
usually the highest-value line item in the whole decision: it deletes a permanent monthly
commitment for life, and it changes the person's relationship to work from hunted to choosing.
Size it as monthly instalment × 12 = annual burn permanently removed.

## 11. What not to do

- Do not say "take it" or "don't take it" before knowing whether a door exists. The
  recommendation is conditional and that condition *is* the answer.
- Do not present the gross package as the number that matters. The runway is the number that
  matters — net package ÷ real monthly burn, in months.
- Do not reason from the package alone. Identical arithmetic yields opposite advice at different
  burn rates and dependant horizons; ask for both before recommending.
- Do not launder the decision as purely financial. Where the person's family history contains no
  model of voluntary exit, "people in our family work until they cannot" arrives as an inherited
  voice quoted as their own judgement. Name it as a voice, not as a fact about them.
- Do not supply a burn rate you were not given. If the person has not stated monthly expenses,
  give ranges across plausible values and say which number would sharpen it.

## 12. Working from the person's own record — dated testimony, not live state

Principals often keep their own notes, dossiers or audits of their situation. Those notes are
tempting evidence — they hold the numbers, the org chart, the clause text and the person's own
conclusions — and they must be handled as **dated testimony**, never as current fact.

- **Re-verify status before reasoning from any archived note.** A note written weeks or months
  earlier records what was true then: a form cycle that has since closed, a restructuring window
  that has finished, a dispute that has been settled, a deadline that has passed. Read the date
  first, list what has changed since, then work from the present. Reasoning from a stale note
  produces advice that is confidently wrong about the world the person is living in — and they
  will feel it at once, because they are the one who has moved.
- **Never quote the person's own past writing back at them to prove they are inconsistent.** When
  someone asks to be challenged, the challenge is about the decision in front of them, not about
  their earlier words. Using their record as a cudgel converts a decision problem into a
  credibility fight and forfeits the trust the record was shared under. Cite their documents to
  *locate* the position ("your note from <period> put the exit trigger at X; that trigger has
  since moved"), then test the position against today's facts.
- **Name what the record cannot settle.** Four things defeat every archive: whether an option
  will still be open later, what an unchanged-but-stagnant employer will do, the employer's own
  internal timeline, and anything the person never wrote down. State these as unresolved rather
  than filling them with whichever narrative is most recent.

### Separate the two decisions

A separation decision is usually two decisions wearing one question. The error is answering the
second with the logic of the first:

| Decision | Nature |
|---|---|
| Should I keep working here? | Strategic, slow; about the role and the employer's future |
| Should I file the interest/intent form? | Tactical, dated; about buying an option |

- **Filing is buying an option, not resigning.** An interest form, questionnaire or register entry
  is a request to be considered, and the employer normally retains discretion to accept or decline
  (confirm against the instrument itself). Filing is therefore *not* the decision to leave, and a
  doctrine that governs leaving must not be used to veto filing.
- **Apply the principal's own standing doctrine to the decision it was written for.** Where the
  person has a rule (for example, that an exit must not be a derivative of a collapse forecast),
  apply it to the *strategic* decision. Do not retarget a strategic rule at a dated, reversible,
  option-purchasing step — that is using their own words to block them, and it reads as a trap.
- **Price the file-and-be-refused case on its own.** A form filed and declined leaves the person
  still employed and now on record as having raised a hand. That social cost is real, appears in
  no figure, and only the person can size it. Ask.
- **Do not accept an unsourced precise statistic into the record.** Numbers that arrive with
  several significant figures and no author, platform, date or link (a study with 1,412 mentions
  and 135,000 engagements, and no provenance) are decoration wearing the costume of evidence.
  Precise figures are cheap to invent and expensive to check, which is exactly why a fabricated
  one looks authoritative. Mark it unverified in the same breath, and say that the real facts
  already carry the argument.
