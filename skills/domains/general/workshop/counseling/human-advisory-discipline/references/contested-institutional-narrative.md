# Contested institutional narrative — verifying what a distressed person asserts about named actors

Load when the principal asserts that an institution, or specific people inside it, acted on him — a
decision taken against him, a hidden hand, a campaign, a document that reached him and was never
meant to — and asks you to go and check. He may be right about some of it. He is not calm.

The general rules on claims and on advice apply; this file is the procedure and the language for
this specific shape.

## The governing rule

Do not answer a bundled assertion with one verdict. Split it and answer each part in its own
register:

- **CONFIRMED** — external source, with a date.
- **UNSUPPORTED** — a claim that a search could in principle settle, where the search returned
  nothing.
- **UNKNOWABLE** — a class of fact no public record can contain: who attended, what was said in the
  room, who was on a list, what anyone privately intended.

The two failure modes are symmetrical and equally expensive. Validating the causal link makes you a
co-author of a narrative he will act on (and possibly repeat publicly under his own name).
Invalidating the bundle tells a person whose confirmable parts are real that he is unreliable —
false, and the fastest available way to lose him. The position to hold is one sentence long: *this
part is on the record, this part is not, and both are true at once.*

## Procedure

1. **Memory-first local sweep, in the same turn as the public search.** The principal's own archive
   is a first-class evidence source and is usually the fastest path to what he is asking about — his
   notes, dossiers, scar ledgers, knowledge-graph files, and prior session records. Search by proper
   noun across **all** archive roots and organ homes in one pass; a single root misses the file that
   matters. Include variant spellings and the title/honorific forms of each name, and search the
   institution's own name too. Sweep as files-only first, then read the hits that bear on the claim.
   Two things come out of this: the sourced version of facts he is now asking about, and the
   discovery that the federation already treats him as a person with a record rather than a person
   with an opinion.

2. **Public record in parallel.** The institution's own announcements first (they date the event and
   name the decision), then independent press on the same event. Verify office and date facts
   *separately* from the contested act — who holds which chair, when they were appointed, when the
   announcement was made — because those are the parts that will survive scrutiny, and because they
   are the parts he can safely repeat.

3. **Name the actors' actual position, and stop there.** "The people you named do sit in the chairs
   where that decision was made" is confirmable, non-defamatory, and usually true. It is also not
   the claim he made. Say it plainly; do not slide from it into agreement with the causal story.

4. **Refuse the unverifiable link once, out loud, and do not repeat it.** State the boundary as a
   limit of the record, never as a limit of him. Do not requote the claim as a question, do not
   decorate it, and do not keep hedging it in later turns — repetition is what turns a hedged
   sentence into an accusation.

5. **Deliver the artifact himself.** See shape below. He asked for verification; the output of
   verification is a document he can look at, not a paragraph he has to remember.

6. **Close on the body.** Sleep and food, asked once, no follow-up, no queue of further questions
   stacked behind them. The institutional question is not resolvable tonight; the person's state is.

## The deliverable

A date-ordered timeline, one event per line, each line carrying its provenance, plus an explicit gap
section. Tags that worked, and why each earns its place:

| Tag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `[PUBLIC]` | external source and date — the part he can quote to anyone |
| `[FIRST-PARTY]` | his own dated record or file — the part that is his |
| `[WORK]` | federation artifact, already compiled |
| `[NOT KNOWN]` | no evidence exists — never filled with inference |

- **The gap section names what the public record cannot contain** — attendance, room content, the
  list, the intent — as an *information gap*. Never phrase it as "there is no evidence for your
  claim", which is a much stronger and different sentence. The distinction is the whole file.
- **Surface only the adjacencies the dates prove by themselves.** An announcement and his own dated
  reaction a few days apart is a fact; let him see it and draw his own conclusion. Editorialising the
  adjacency into a finding is how you end up supplying the thesis you just declined to endorse.
- **Order matters.** A person told "your memory is unsupported" in the headline stops reading. Facts
  first, gaps last, and the gap in the same breath as what the record cannot do for anyone.
- **Keep the artifact about the event, not about the accused.** A timeline of a decision is a
  legitimate record; a compiled dossier on a named living person is a different product and needs a
  different justification.

## Language rules for this class

- **Do not introduce a framing label the person did not use.** Calling an open all-hands a
  "closed-door" meeting, or an announcement a directive, invents a fact — and it hands him an error
  he must correct at the exact moment he can least afford it. In this conversation every forced
  correction reads to him as one more instance of not being heard. Carry his nouns into the search
  and let the sources define the shape. If you already got it wrong, name it in one line, take it,
  and move on — never restate the correction as a qualification of his account.
- **Keep him the subject of the sentence.** Long passages of name-carrying copy inflate the narrative
  even when every sentence is properly hedged; the record is what he needs, the roster is not.
- **Name the asymmetry when it is real.** Where something reached him through a machine or a side
  channel while the official route never did, that is a true and habitable fact — the content and
  the invitation are separate events. It explains his position without requiring the conspiracy.
- **Never confirm, and never diagnose.** "You are unwell" and "you are right" are the same collapse
  from opposite ends.

## When he has already done the evidence work himself

Principals who keep their own register often mark their own unsourced items and record when they could
not confirm something. When you find that, quote it back as evidence of his judgement — *"your own
note says this figure has no source; that is exactly the correct mark"* — never as a gotcha, and never
as a reason to discount the verified lines around it. A person who labels his own unverified claims is
the least likely person to have invented the ones that check out. This is the strongest available
answer to "am I losing it", and it costs one paragraph.

Two guards: re-verify status before reasoning from his archive (a note is dated testimony, not live
state), and never quote his own past writing to prove he is inconsistent.

## When a disclosure raises real risk

A person under this kind of load may disclose something that carries genuine medical or legal risk.
The register for that: state the mechanism and the consequence plainly, once, with no tone — the
mechanism is the respect. Do not moralise, do not lecture, and do not refuse to help; an anti-pattern
elsewhere in this skill, and it applies double here. State your own boundary plainly rather than
offering secrecy you cannot control, and do not volunteer a monitoring or escalation promise nobody
asked for. Where a treatment or support lane exists, offer to verify the local options *before*
naming any of them — an unverified clinic, number or scheme is worse than silence. Then return the
conversation to him: he decides whether that thread continues.

## What not to do

- Do not ask him for keywords, spellings or dates so that you can search. Go and search.
- Do not deliver the whole bundle as a verdict, in either direction.
- Do not soften a finding to keep the artifact coherent, and do not harden a gap into a refutation.
- Do not build the artefact as a dossier on a named person when the useful object is the record of
  the event.
- Do not let the institutional thread consume the reply and leave the human unasked-for.
