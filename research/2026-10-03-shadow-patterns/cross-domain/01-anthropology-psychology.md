# PART A — ANTHROPOLOGY

## A1. Premature commitment: when a community commits to an interpretation before the evidence arrives

### A1.1 — Malinowski, Trobriand magic scales with uncertainty, not with competence

- **Citation:** Malinowski, B. (1925/1948). "Magic, Science and Religion." In *Magic, Science and Religion and Other Essays*. Free Press. Fieldwork basis: *Coral Gardens and Their Magic* (1935), Vol. I: *Soil-Tilling and Agricultural Rites in the Trobriand Islands*; and "Fishing in the Trobriand Islands" (*Man* / JSTOR 2788612). Full-text Vol. I: https://www.berose.fr/IMG/pdf/malinowski_1935-coral_gardens-vol_1.pdf
- **Claim (OBS):** Trobrianders used **no magic for lagoon fishing** — safe, predictable, skill-determined — and **elaborate ritual for open-sea fishing**, which was dangerous and its yield uncertain. The ethnographic finding, corroborated across every secondary source located, is that ritual intensity tracks **irreducible uncertainty and danger**, not the actor's competence. Malinowski reasoned magic "was not used while fishing in the lagoon because there was little danger and your ability to catch fish was based" on skill.
- **Relevance:** This is the cleanest anthropological anchor for premature commitment available, and it is **inverted** from the agent case. Human societies built a *scripted commitment procedure* precisely where evidence was structurally unavailable — and withheld it where evidence was obtainable by direct observation. The coding-agent failure is the reverse deployment: scripted action is invoked where evidence **is** obtainable (the repo), and restraint is absent where it is not. Malinowski supplies the boundary condition: ritual commitment is a *substitute* for evidence, legitimate only where evidence cannot arrive in time.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (corroborated primary-adjacent: JSTOR abstract, full-text Vol. I PDF located, ≥4 independent secondary confirmations).

### A1.2 — Evans-Pritchard, Azande oracle consultation as a scripted interpretation-lock

- **Citation:** Evans-Pritchard, E. E. (1937/1976). *Witchcraft, Oracles, and Magic among the Azande*. Oxford: Clarendon Press. Full-text excerpt incl. the chapter "Problems Arising from Consultation": https://dougbafford.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Evans-Pritchard-1937-1976.pdf
- **Claim (INT):** The Azande benge (poison) oracle was not a guess about the future but a **procedure that converted an unanswerable question — who is the witch? — into a binary verdict the community could act on**. The oracle's authority rested on the correctness of the question posed and the ritual conditions of the consultation; Evans-Pritchard devotes a chapter to what goes wrong when consultation procedure is defective. Crucially, a **failed oracle did not discredit the system** — failure was explained by a breach of procedure (wrong poison, taboo violation, a rival oracle's interference), never by the theory being false.
- **Relevance:** Two independent anchors for two different patterns. For **premature commitment**: the oracle fixes an interpretation *before* any confirming evidence exists, and the community then acts on it. For **narrative completion**: the exemption clause structure — every disconfirming observation is absorbed as a procedural defect rather than a theory failure — is the ethnographic original of what Anthropic's tracing paper calls "working backwards to find a plausible intermediate step." The Azande case shows this is not a defect of reasoning but a *load-bearing feature* of a system that must produce actionable verdicts under permanent uncertainty.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (chapter-level excerpt read from full-text PDF).

### A1.3 — Festinger, Riecken & Schachter: disconfirmed prophecy increases conviction

- **Citation:** Festinger, L., Riecken, H. W., & Schachter, S. (1956). *When Prophecy Fails: A Social and Psychological Study of a Modern Group That Predicted the Destruction of the World*. University of Minnesota Press. Full text: https://ia802802.us.archive.org/4/items/pdfy-eDNpDzTy_dR1b0iB/Festinger-Riecken-Schachter-When-Prophecy-Fails-1956.pdf
- **Claim (OBS):** Field observation of a group that predicted a specific date of world destruction. When the prophecy failed, the group did **not** dissolve and its members did **not** abandon belief. Conviction *increased*, and proselytizing effort *increased* — the group sought new adherents precisely after disconfirmation. Cognitive dissonance theory was derived from this observation.
- **Relevance:** The strongest available human anchor for **narrative completion under disconfirmation**, and it supplies the mechanism the AI literature lacks: commitment is not merely sticky, it is *socially load-bearing*, so abandoning it costs identity and community, and the cheapest repair is to strengthen the narrative rather than revise it. This is structurally the same shape as an agent that has already emitted a diagnosis and then produces additional reasoning to defend it rather than retracting.
- **⚠️ Honesty flag:** the evidentiary basis of this classic is contested. Kelly, R. (2026). "Debunking 'When Prophecy Fails'." *Journal of the History of the Behavioral Sciences*, doi:10.1002/jhbs.70043 — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/jhbs.70043. Cite the *finding* as historically influential, not as settled.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (full-text PDF located) + contested-status flag from the 2026 reassessment.

### A1.4 — Van Gennep / Turner: rites of passage make commitment structurally irreversible

- **Citation:** Van Gennep, A. (1909/1960). *The Rites of Passage* (trans. Vizedom & Caffee). Chicago UP. Turner, V. (1969). *The Ritual Process: Structure and Anti-Structure*. Aldine. Representative modern applications read: *Rites of Passage and Healing Efficacy* (pmid 19513913); *The Rites of Passage Framework as a Matrix of Transgression Processes in the Life Course* (PMC6105198); *Adverse Events as Transitional Markers* (pmid 33316570).
- **Claim (DER):** The universal three-phase structure — **separation → liminality/marginality → reaggregation** — is a *designed* commitment device. The initiate is removed from the prior status before the outcome is known, and the liminal phase is characterized by structural indeterminacy ("betwixt and between", Turner). Return is not an available move; the only exits are completion or failure-to-reaggregate, which the framework literature treats as a pathological stuck state.
- **Relevance:** For **premature commitment**: the anthropological insight is that irrevocability is often *the point*, not the accident. A rite commits the community to an interpretation of who this person now is before any evidence of transformation exists, and it deliberately destroys the option to un-commit — because an optional commitment is not a commitment. This separates two things the AI literature conflates: commitment that is premature *because evidence was skipped*, versus commitment that is early *by design because reversibility would defeat its function*. Coding agents currently have the timing of the second without the warrant of the first.
- **Receipt:** `HYPOTHESIS` for the primary texts (not read directly); `VERIFIED` for the framework structure via multiple applied papers read in abstract.

### A1.5 — Leeson: the judicial ordeal as an information-revealing institution

- **Citation:** Leeson, P. T. (2012). "Ordeals." *Journal of Law and Economics* 55(3):691–714. Also Leeson, P. T. (2014). "Oracles." *Rationality and Society* 26(3), doi:10.1177/1043463113512997. Abstract via ResearchGate 225844047.
- **Claim (DER):** Leeson argues — using rational choice theory — that medieval judicial ordeals **accurately assigned guilt and innocence**, not because God intervened but because the procedure functioned as a **self-selection mechanism**: an innocent defendant who genuinely believed in the ordeal's divine backing would submit; a guilty one would settle, flee, or confess rather than face it. The ritual's *believed* mechanism was not its *operative* mechanism.
- **Relevance:** The sharpest anthropological anchor for the gap between **stated justification and operative function** — and thus for **narrative completion**. A community ran a truth-finding procedure whose declared reasoning was false and whose actual effectiveness came from an entirely different channel nobody articulated. The parallel to unfaithful chain-of-thought is exact in structure: the emitted justification and the computation that produced the verdict are different objects. Leeson also supplies the missing piece the AI literature lacks — such a system can be *effective* while its self-description is *false*, and it decays only when belief in the declared mechanism erodes.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (abstract read; two Leeson papers cross-confirmed for title, year, venue, pagination).

## A2. Authority / legitimation: how societies distinguish CAN from MAY

### A2.1 — Weber: authority is a claim to legitimacy, not a capacity

- **Citation:** Weber, M. (1922). *Wirtschaft und Gesellschaft*. Standard English excerpt: "The Three Types of Legitimate Rule." Read via Yale OYC SOCY 151 Lecture 17 (https://oyc.yale.edu/sociology/socy-151/lecture-17) and the *Tripartite classification of authority* article.
- **Claim (DER):** Weber separates **power** (*Macht* — the probability of imposing one's will despite resistance) from **authority/domination** (*Herrschaft* — the probability that a command will be obeyed, resting on a **belief in its legitimacy**). Legitimacy is claimed on exactly three grounds: **traditional** ("it has always been so"), **charismatic** (devotion to an exceptional individual), and **legal-rational** (enacted rules and the office that holds them). Obedience is owed to the *office*, not the person, under legal-rational rule.
- **Relevance:** The single most important anchor for the **authority gap**, because Weber's distinction is *not* capability-vs-permission — it is **coercion-vs-legitimacy**. An actor with maximum capability and no legitimacy claim is exercising *power*, which for Weber is a different social object requiring different maintenance (continuous enforcement) and producing different failure modes (collapse the moment enforcement lapses). A coding agent that edits files because nothing stopped it is in Weber's *Macht* category, not *Herrschaft*. Legal-rational authority additionally locates legitimacy in the **office and its rules**, not in the officeholder's demonstrated skill — which is precisely why expertise cannot confer authority, and why an agent's fluency (charismatic-adjacent) is the wrong basis for a mutation grant.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (two independent structural summaries read; primary text not read directly).

### A2.2 — Hart: power-conferring rules produce *nullity*, not violation

- **Citation:** Hart, H. L. A. (1961). *The Concept of Law*. Oxford: Clarendon Press. Secondary read: EBSCO Research Starters entry; "Power-Conferring Laws and the Rule of Recognition" (SSRN 3116865); "Hart on Legal Powers as Legal Competences," *Canadian Journal of Law and Jurisprudence* (https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/canadian-journal-of-law-and-jurisprudence/article/hart-on-legal-powers-as-legal-competences/6BEE4F5C9DC6C67D170764C0CB39C076); "Did H.L.A. Hart, of All People, Neglect Power-Conferring Laws?" (juris.jotwell.com).
- **Claim (DER):** Hart's central move against Austin's command theory is that **not all rules impose duties**. Some rules **confer powers** — to make a will, sign a contract, marry, legislate. Crucially, acting without the conferred power is **not a breach of duty and attracts no sanction**; the act is simply **void**. Separately, Hart distinguishes **"being obliged"** (coerced by threat) from **"being under an obligation"** (subject to a rule one has reason to accept).
- **Relevance:** This is the cleanest formal CAN-vs-MAY distinction in the entire literature and it is *not* in the AI governance material reviewed. A duty-imposing rule answers "must I not?" and its violation is detectable after the fact. A power-conferring rule answers "may I?" and its absence makes the act **inert at the moment of attempted execution** — there is nothing to detect afterwards. The arifOS F13-class distinction (capability↑ ⇒ authority∅) is structurally Hart's secondary rules. Hart also supplies the reason **coercion is the wrong model**: an agent that refrains only because a sanitizer blocked it is "being obliged"; an agent that refrains because the grant is absent is "being under an obligation." These have different failure modes — the first fails open when the blocker has a gap, the second fails closed.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (four secondary sources read incl. two peer-reviewed; primary not read directly).

### A2.3 — Roman augural law: a runtime authority gate with veto-over-in-progress

- **Citation:** Smith, W. (1875). *A Dictionary of Greek and Roman Antiquities*, s.v. "Augurium." Full text: https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/secondary/SMIGRA*/Augurium.html. Also "The Nature and Function of Auspicia in Ancient Roman Religion and Politics" (https://brewminate.com/the-nature-and-function-of-auspicia-in-ancient-roman-religion-and-politics/); Linderski, J., "On the Dangers of Ignoring Auspices" (academia.edu 111979407); *The Challenge to the Auspices: Studies on Magisterial Power in the Middle Republic* (BMCR 2023.12.18).
- **Claim (OBS):** The dictionary text is unambiguous: **"no public act could be performed without consulting the auspices, no election could be held, no law passed, no war waged"**; neglect was grounds for invalidating the act. The magistrate held *potestas* and often *imperium* — full capability to command — but the *auspicium* was a separate held asset. And the augurs **"had the power to interrupt an assembly once it had started by declaring ill omens"** (*obnuntiatio*).
- **Relevance:** The best single historical case of an operating society that **refused to let capability imply authority**, and it is stronger than anything in the AI governance literature because it shows the mechanism at runtime. Rome had: (a) a *separate office* holding the permission-granting asset, independent of the executing office; (b) a gate **before** execution, not an audit after; (c) the ability to **halt an action already underway** — not a periodic review. The BMCR volume's note that "undesired auspices in certain cases were also rejected or ignored by the respective magistrates" supplies the failure mode: the gate decayed exactly when the executing office gained enough capability to override it. That is the *authority gap* as a documented historical process, with a named decay mechanism.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (three independent sources read, incl. primary-reference full text).

### A2.4 — Douglas: taboo as a classification-enforcement system, and a post-hoc detector

- **Citation:** Douglas, M. (1966/2001). *Purity and Danger: An Analysis of Concepts of Pollution and Taboo*. Routledge. Full text: https://monoskop.org/images/7/7d/Douglas_Mary_Purity_and_Danger_An_Analysis_of_Concepts_of_Pollution_and_Taboo_2001.pdf
- **Claim (INT):** Douglas's thesis: **"dirt is matter out of place."** Pollution beliefs are not primitive hygiene but the enforcement layer of a classification system — they mark the boundaries of categories and punish category-crossing. Two findings from the text read are directly load-bearing. First, **"pollution rules do not correspond closely to moral rules"** — some acts judged wrong provoke no pollution belief, and some acts "not thought very reprehensible are held to be polluting and dangerous." The two systems are *separate*. Second, among the Nuer, where kinship is distant and detection unlikely, "they can leave the matter to be settled post hoc by the appearance or non-appearance of the skin disease" — pollution belief **"acts as a post hoc detector of the crime,"** and its social function is **"marshalling moral disapproval when it lags."**
- **Relevance:** Two distinct anchors. The **separation of pollution rules from moral rules** is the anthropological version of separating MAY from SHOULD: a society can have an operative permission system that is not a moral system and does not derive from one. An agent's authority boundary is a pollution rule, not a moral claim — and treating it as the latter is what produces the "confident mistake" pattern, where the agent justifies an unpermitted act on grounds of correctness. The **post-hoc detector** function is the more striking find: Douglas documents societies that *deliberately deferred adjudication* because pre-emptive judgment was unavailable — the exact opposite of premature commitment, and an institutional design that tolerates a delayed verdict rather than manufacturing an early one.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (substantial full-text excerpt read, spanning Introduction through "Internal Lines").

### A2.5 — French & Raven: five bases of power, and the fluency/legitimacy split

- **Citation:** French, J. R. P. & Raven, B. (1959). "The Bases of Social Power." In Cartwright (ed.), *Studies in Social Power*. Ann Arbor: Institute for Social Research. Read via https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_and_Raven%27s_bases_of_power and ResearchGate 215915730. Raven, B. (1965) added a sixth base (informational).
- **Claim (DER):** Social influence is defined as **"a change in the belief, attitude, or behavior of a person (the target of influence) which results from the action of another person (an influencing agent)"**; social power is **"the potential for such influence."** Five bases: **coercive, reward, legitimate, referent, expert**.
- **Relevance:** The taxonomy matters because the bases are **not interchangeable and do not transfer**. The coding-agent case is a clean instance of **expert power without legitimate power** — the agent is persuasive because its output reads as competent, and it inherits no office. French & Raven's framework predicts the failure: expert power operates on *belief change*, while legitimate power operates on *recognized jurisdiction*. An agent that converts expert power into de facto jurisdiction is doing something the taxonomy says should not be possible, which is exactly why the resulting actions are unreviewable — there is no office to hold accountable.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (definition and five bases read verbatim from two sources; primary chapter not read).

### A2.6 — Hofstede: power distance, with the standard critiques attached

- **Citation:** Hofstede, G. (1980). *Culture's Consequences*. Sage. Operational definition from the author's own site: https://geerthofstede.com/culture-geert-hofstede-gert-jan-hofstede/6d-model-of-national-culture/. Overview: ScienceDirect Topics, "Power Distance."
- **Claim (OBS/DER):** Power Distance is **"the extent to which the less powerful members of organizations and institutions (like the family) accept and expect that power is distributed unequally."** Note the construct measures *acceptance and expectation by the less powerful* — not the amount of power the powerful hold.
- **Relevance:** The relevant move for the authority gap is that Hofstede locates authority in the **subordinate's acceptance**, which makes authority a property of the relationship rather than of the actor. In high-PDI contexts "employees tend not to question their superiors' orders, often feeling afraid and unwilling to express disagreement"; role-holders (doctors, lawyers, professors) are "held in high esteem." Applied to agents: the *user's* disposition, not the agent's design, determines whether an unpermitted action gets challenged. An agent deployed into a high-PDI working relationship faces no effective runtime objection, so the only authority gate that will hold is one that does not depend on the human noticing.
- **⚠️ Honesty flags:** (a) McSweeney, B. (2002), "Hofstede's Model of National Cultural Differences and their Consequences," *Human Relations* — sampling critique, national-level aggregation illegitimate for individual inference. (b) "Re-Examining the Validity of Hofstede's Power Distance Dimension," *Journal of Intercultural Communication* 26(1), doi:10.36923/jicc.v26i1.1312 — https://immi.se/index.php/intercultural/article/view/10.36923.jicc.v26i1.1312: cross-country differences **persist but are less pronounced than the original rankings suggest**. Use the construct, not the national scores.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (author's own definition read; two critiques located and abstracted).

## A3. "Thinking without thinking": hunter-gatherer decision-making under uncertainty

### A3.1 — Tracked foragers gather *more* evidence precisely when the first interpretation fails

- **Citation:** "High-precision tracking of human foragers reveals adaptive social information use in the wild." *Science* (2026), doi:10.1126/science.ady1055, pmid:41610226. Companion preprint: "Tracking human foragers and their prey reveals adaptive predator-prey dynamics," doi:10.64898/2026.09.21.752541.
- **Claim (OBS):** Direct GPS-scale observation of foragers in the wild. Finding: **"Foragers adaptively rely on social information to locate resources when unsuccessful and extend giving-up times in the presence of others, which results in increased"** return. The companion paper: "foragers adaptively combined personal and social information in deciding where to forage and for how long."
- **Relevance:** This is the most important **counter-anchor** in the whole anthropology lane, and it should be weighted accordingly. It is direct field evidence that the human default under a failed first interpretation is **not** to commit harder — it is to *extend the evidence-gathering window* and to recruit information from other agents. The trigger is explicitly conditional on **unsuccess**. Premature commitment is therefore not a human universal being inherited by agents; it is a specific failure of the conditional. Whatever suppresses the "I was unsuccessful, therefore widen the search" reflex in an agent is the actual object of study, and this paper shows the reflex is real, measurable, and load-bearing in the species the training data came from.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (abstracts read for both).

### A3.2 — Mikea of Madagascar: field-embedded humans outperform lab humans on covariation

- **Citation:** Tucker, B. et al. "Perception of Interannual Covariation and Strategies for Risk Reduction among Mikea of Madagascar: Individual and Social Learning." *Human Nature* 18, doi:10.1007/s12110-007-9007-z, pmid:26181848.
- **Claim (OBS):** A historical matrix participatory exercise showed **"Mikea perceive that foraging and farming outcomes covary positively or negatively with rainfall."** The paper's own contrast is explicit: **"In contrast, the psychological literature suggests that individuals judge covariation poorly when memory of past"** events is the only data source.
- **Relevance:** This is the necessary correction to the naive System-1/System-2 mapping the AI literature tends to import. The standard framing — "hunter-gatherers run on fast intuition, WEIRD lab subjects run on slow analysis" — is not supported here. On a task that is *ecologically embedded and consequential*, foragers tracked multi-year covariation accurately where laboratory subjects systematically fail. Fast and slow are not attributes of populations; they are attributes of **task–environment fit**. Any claim that coding agents "inherit human fast-thinking bias" needs this caveat: the bias shows up in the lab precisely because the lab strips the ecological structure that makes human judgment accurate.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (abstract read, including the explicit contrast with the psychology literature).

### A3.3 — Martu and Hadza: high-variance pursuit as a *social* strategy, not a misjudged one

- **Citation:** Bird, D. W., Bird, R. B., & Codding, B. F. "To kill a kangaroo: understanding the decision to pursue high-risk/high-gain resources." *Proc. R. Soc. B* 280:20131210, PMC3735252. Also "Why women hunt: risk and contemporary foraging in a Western Desert aboriginal community" (pmid 19230267), and "Hunting income patterns among the Hadza" (Blumhoff/ Hawkes, *Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B* 346, pmid 1685582).
- **Claim (OBS/DER):** Martu and Hadza hunters pursue prey with **high mean *and* high variance** — daily returns are erratic, and expected-utility frameworks based on caloric mean alone predict they should not. "To kill a kangaroo" resolves this using an expected utility framework with variance-sensitive terms; "Why women hunt" finds gendered asymmetry in foraging decisions "is linked to men's and women's different **social** strategies. Men trade off reliable consumption benefits to the hunter's" — i.e. the return being optimized is partly social capital, not calories. The Hadza data show hunters "disregard small prey in favour of larger forms" even though including small animals would raise mean daily rate.
- **Relevance:** Anchors a distinction the agent literature lacks entirely: **an apparently suboptimal action can be optimal against a different objective function than the observer assumed.** The foragers are not misjudging; they are optimizing for a return channel (sharing, status, alliance) that the calorie-only model cannot see. Applied to coding agents: the "action bias" may be optimal against the training objective (visible helpfulness) while pathological against the deployment objective (restraint), and this is not a bug in the agent's judgment but a mismatch in the objective the analyst assumed. It also gives the anthropological warrant for reading a persistent "irrational" behavior as evidence of an unmodeled payoff rather than as noise.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (abstracts read for all three).

### A3.4 — Dual-process theory, with its contestation

- **Citation:** Evans, J. St. B. T. & Stanovich, K. E. (2013). "In two minds: dual-process accounts of reasoning." *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 17(5):229–238, PMC5378157. Clinical application read: "Cognitive bias: how understanding its impact on antibiotic prescribing decisions can help advance antimicrobial stewardship," *JAC-Antimicrobial Resistance* (2021), PMC8210114.
- **Claim (DER):** Dual-process accounts posit judgments mediated by both **fast, automatic (Type 1)** and **slower, deliberative, analytic (Type 2)** processes. The clinical framing is the useful one for throughput: "In the healthcare setting where thousands of decisions are made daily, **Type 1 thinking can reduce**" cognitive load — the trade is explicit, not a defect.
- **Relevance:** Supplies the mechanism by which premature commitment is *rational under load*: Type 1 is not a failure of Type 2, it is the only mode that fits the decision budget. An agent with a context window and a turn budget is in exactly the position of a clinician with a full waiting room — which means premature commitment is a predictable output of throughput pressure, not of model quality.
- **⚠️ Honesty flags:** dual-process theory is actively contested. "Intuitive and deliberate judgments are based on common principles" (*JPSP*, pmid 21244188) argues the associative/rule-based dichotomy is overstated. "Fast logic?: Examining the time course assumption of dual process theory" (*Cognition*, pmid 27816844) challenges the speed ordering directly. "Conflict monitoring in dual process theories of thinking" (*Cognition*, pmid 17631876). Cite as a useful frame with known disputes, not as settled architecture.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (abstracts read) + three contesting papers located.

# PART B — PSYCHOLOGY

## B1. Premature commitment → premature closure (the human mirror)

### B1.1 — Croskerry: the canonical taxonomy, and premature closure as the flagship error

- **Citation:** Croskerry, P. (2003). "The Importance of Cognitive Errors in Diagnosis and Strategies to Minimize Them." ***Academic Medicine* 78(8):775–780.** pmid:12915363. Full text: https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/uzzi/ftp/restricted/articles/Cognitive%20Errors%20in%20Diagnosis%20-%20Academic%20Medicine%20-%202003.pdf · Also Croskerry, P. (2002). "Achieving Quality in Clinical Decision Making: Cognitive Strategies and Detection of Bias," *Academic Emergency Medicine*; and "Cognitive debiasing; part 1 and part 2" (AHRQ PSNet).
- **Claim (OBS/DER):** "A critical subset of diagnostic errors arises through cognitive errors, especially those associated with **failures in perception, failed heuristics, and biases**." Croskerry enumerates **30+ "cognitive dispositions to respond" (CDRs)** — stable tendencies, not knowledge deficits — of which premature closure is the most-cited. He proposes **cognitive forcing strategies** and **metacognition/debiasing** as countermeasures.
- **Relevance:** The definitional source for premature closure, and its framing of CDRs as **dispositions** rather than errors is the transferable part. A disposition is a *tendency that fires under conditions*, which means it is predictable and gate-able — you do not need to know which case will trigger it, only which conditions make it likely. This is the human precedent for treating premature commitment as a property of the decision environment rather than of the decider's quality.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (abstract read, full-text PDF located, pagination corrected against a bad secondary cite).

### B1.2 — Kassirer & Kopelman: premature closure quantified, and independent of training level

- **Citation:** Kassirer, J. P. & Kopelman, R. I. (1986). "Premature conclusions in the diagnosis of iron-deficiency anemia: cause and effect." *Medical Decision Making* 6(3), doi:10.1177/0272989X8600600307, pmid:3736379.
- **Claim (OBS):** In **24 of 35 cases (69%)**, "the diagnostic conclusion seemed unjustified and characteristic of premature closure, one of four recurring errors in diagnostic reasoning described previously." And: **"Premature closure appeared to originate from subjects at all levels of training."**
- **Relevance:** Two things at once. The 69% figure is the human baseline for how often a confident conclusion outruns its evidence in a *narrow, well-specified* diagnostic domain. The "all levels of training" finding is more important for the agent case: **more capability does not reduce premature closure.** This directly undercuts the implicit assumption in most agent-safety writing that a stronger model will commit less prematurely. The human data says the opposite — see B1.3.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (abstract read).

### B1.3 — Experience *increases* susceptibility to premature closure

- **Citation:** "The difficulty with experience: does practice increase susceptibility to premature closure?" *Continuing Education in the Health Professions* 26(3), doi:10.1002/chp.69, pmid:16986144. Corroborating: "Avoiding premature closure and reaching diagnostic accuracy: some key predictive factors," *Medical Education* 51(11), doi:10.1111/medu.13382, pmid:28857266; "Experienced physicians benefit from analyzing initial diagnostic hypotheses," PMC4563663.
- **Claim (OBS):** The paper's framing statement: **"A recent review of the physician performance literature concluded that the risk of prematurely closing one's diagnostic search increases with years of experience."** The study was designed to test this while minimizing confounds. PMC4563663 states independently: **"Most incorrect diagnoses involve at least one cognitive error, of which premature closure is the most prevalent."** And 28857266: "Early studies suggested that experienced clinicians simply generate more accurate diagnoses than those less experienced. **However, more recent studies indicate that experienced clinicians may be subject to biases in formulating and confirming hypotheses**."
- **Relevance:** The single most consequential finding in this slice for the agent case, because it inverts the capability→safety assumption. Pattern recognition *is* the competence; premature closure is its shadow. An expert closes fast because fast closure is usually right — which is exactly why it is not caught when it is wrong. A coding agent that has been trained to produce confident, fluent, correct-looking patches has been trained into the same trade, and there is no reason from this literature to expect scale to reduce it. The mitigation that *does* appear in the literature is structural, not capability-based: PMC4563663 finds experienced physicians benefit specifically from **analyzing their initial hypotheses** — an explicit second pass over the first commitment, not more skill.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (three abstracts read, all converging).

### B1.4 — Premature closure reproduced in an RCT, 2026

- **Citation:** "Premature closure underlies bias in medical diagnosis in students: A randomised controlled experiment." *Medical Education* (2026), doi:10.1111/medu.70229, pmid:42051011.
- **Claim (OBS):** The paper sets out two competing mechanistic hypotheses — **"premature closure leading biased participants to spend less time on diagnosis"** versus **"increased competition between diagnostic hypotheses"** — and tests them in a randomized controlled design.
- **Relevance:** Establishes that premature closure is still, in 2026, an **open mechanistic question** in its home discipline: does the commitment truncate evidence-gathering, or does it corrupt the weighing of evidence that *does* arrive? The distinction matters enormously for the agent case and has no settled answer in the human literature either. The AI-side papers cited in the base document (Mehta et al., "settle before gathering *or using* evidence") are implicitly picking between these two, and this RCT shows the choice is not yet empirically closed in humans.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (abstract read).

### B1.5 — Groopman, *How Doctors Think* — popular synthesis

- **Citation:** Groopman, J. (2007). *How Doctors Think*. Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 307pp. Wikipedia entry; NYT review 2007-03-27; peer book reviews at PMC2776809 and PMC1994632.
- **Claim (INT):** "Groopman analyzes how physicians come to make diagnostic and treatment decisions, and how this process can be improved upon." Case-history method; attributes frequent misdiagnosis to **cognitive errors rooted in cognitive psychology** rather than to knowledge gaps. PMC1994632: "The explicit purpose of *How doctors think* is to give laypeople an understanding of the medical mind so that they can participate more actively."
- **Relevance:** Useful as the accessible statement of the premature-closure thesis and as evidence the pattern is legible enough to a general audience to be a bestseller — which is itself a datum about how *named* the failure mode is. But it is a **trade book, not primary research**, and it should not carry evidential weight in the review. Use Croskerry (B1.1) and Kassirer & Kopelman (B1.2) for the claim; use Groopman only for the framing that clinicians' errors are cognitive rather than informational.

### B1.6 — Endpoint diagnoses as a cognitive forcing strategy

- **Citation:** "Pursuit of 'endpoint diagnoses' as a cognitive forcing strategy to avoid premature diagnostic closure." *Diagnosis* (2022), doi:10.1515/dx-2022-0013, pmid:35942949. Related: "Diagnostic errors and flaws in clinical reasoning: mechanisms and prevention in practice," *Swiss Medical Weekly* 142:w13706, pmid:23135902.
- **Claim (OBS/DER):** "Premature closure is often described as a significant contributor to diagnostic error." The 2022 paper tests a specific **forcing strategy**: require the clinician to name an *endpoint* diagnosis — the underlying mechanism, not the presenting label — before closing. The Swiss review states the mechanism plainly: **"The majority of cognitive errors are not related to knowledge deficiency but to flaws in data collection, data integration, and data verification that may lead to premature diagnostic closure."**
- **Relevance:** This is the one place in the human literature where a *specific, tested structural intervention* against premature closure is reported, and its shape is informative: the fix is not "think longer" or "be more careful" but **changing what counts as a completed answer**. Requiring an endpoint diagnosis raises the bar for closure by redefining the artifact. The Swiss finding supplies the warrant that the lever is procedural, not epistemic — the errors are in collection/integration/verification, i.e. in the *pipeline*, not in the store of knowledge.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (both abstracts read).

## B2. Action bias

### B2.1 — Bar-Eli et al.: the strongest action-bias result, and it is norm-driven

- **Citation:** Bar-Eli, M., Azar, O. H., Ritov, I., Keidar-Levin, Y., & Schein, G. (2007). "Action bias among elite soccer goalkeepers: The case of penalty kicks." *Journal of Economic Psychology* 28(5):606–621. **FULL TEXT READ:** https://pluto.mscc.huji.ac.il/~msiritov/BarEliAzarRitovKeidarSchein.pdf
- **Claim (OBS):** Analysis of **286 penalty kicks** in top leagues and championships worldwide. Given the empirical distribution of kick direction, **the optimal strategy for goalkeepers is to stay in the goal's center.** "Goalkeepers, however, almost always jump right or left." A second study surveyed **32 top professional goalkeepers** and confirmed that jumping is perceived as the norm. Mechanism is explicitly **norm theory** (Kahneman & Miller, 1986, *Psychological Review* 93:136–153): "because the norm is to jump, norm theory implies that a goal scored yields worse feelings for the goalkeeper following inaction (staying in the center) than following action (jumping), leading to a bias for action."
- **Relevance:** Four things this single paper supplies that nothing else in the slice does.
  1. **The bias is directional because the norm is directional.** "The omission bias, a bias in favor of inaction, is reversed here because the norm here is reversed — to act rather than to choose inaction." So action bias is not a primitive; it is *anticipated-regret asymmetry shaped by what the surrounding community treats as the default*. Whatever an agent has been trained to regard as "the normal helpful move" sets the direction of its bias. This is the missing link between training pressure and bias direction.
  2. **Incentives do not fix it.** "The seemingly biased decision making is particularly striking since the goalkeepers have huge incentives to make correct decisions, and it is a decision they encounter frequently." High stakes and high repetition — the two things one would expect to select the bias out — did not.
  3. **It is a decision made before evidence is available.** "Goalkeepers choose their action before they can clearly observe the kick direction." Action bias and premature commitment are the *same temporal failure* in this setting, not two patterns.
  4. The **survey of the goalkeepers themselves** establishes the norm is *endorsed by the actors*, not merely inferred from behavior — the equivalent of an agent's own stated policy agreeing that acting is correct.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` — **full paper text read** (abstract, method, both studies, mechanism, conclusion).

### B2.2 — Patt & Zeckhauser: action bias in environmental decisions

- **Citation:** Patt, A. & Zeckhauser, R. (2000). "Action Bias and Environmental Decisions." *Journal of Risk and Uncertainty* 21(1):45–72. ResearchGate 5152280. Confirmed as a cited reference in ≥6 independent works located (*Journal of Theoretical Politics*, doi:10.1177/0951629814559723; ESSEC HAL-01205913; SSRN 3697232; Texas A&M Law carbon-tax paper; *Cambridge Experimental Economics* "villain's dilemma"; Toulouse WP TSE 766).
- **Claim (DER/OBS):** ResearchGate framing: action bias is **"the tendency to favor visible intervention over restraint, even when inaction or delayed action would produce better outcomes."** The environmental-decisions setting is the paper's contribution: regulators and policymakers facing diffuse, slow-moving risk exhibit a preference for taking visible action.
- **Relevance:** The *Cambridge* "villain's dilemma" citation supplies the sharpest usable gloss: **"Sometimes referred to as action bias (see Patt & Zeckhauser, 2000 for an experimental investigation)."** The phrase **"visible intervention over restraint"** is the operative one for the coding-agent case. Visibility is the key term — the bias is not toward *effective* action but toward action that *can be seen to have been taken*. An agent whose outputs are evaluated as diffs, commits, and reported steps is being scored on visible intervention. This paper is the citation for that mechanism, and it is the one the brief was reaching for.
- **⚠️ Correction recorded:** the brief's "Patt & Zeckhauser 1989, 'Action Bias and Unexpected Returns'" does not exist. Correct year 2000, correct title above.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (title/year/venue/pagination cross-confirmed by six independent citing sources; abstract read via ResearchGate).

### B2.3 — Anderson 2003: the canonical citation is for the *opposite* bias

- **Citation:** Anderson, C. J. (2003). "The psychology of doing nothing: Forms of decision avoidance result from reason and emotion." *Psychological Bulletin* 129(1):139–167, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.129.1.139, pmid:12555797. SSRN 895727 (abstract read in full); PsycNET 2002-11509-008.
- **Claim (DER):** "This review relates findings across several different disciplines and uncovers **4 decision avoidance effects**: choice deferral, status quo bias, omission bias, and inaction inertia. These findings are related by common antecedents and consequences in a **rational-emotional model** of the factors that predispose humans to do nothing. Prominent components of the model include **cost-benefit calculations, anticipated regret, and selection difficulty.**"
- **Relevance:** **The brief misfiles this to the wrong pattern.** Anderson 2003 is *the* canonical review of decision **avoidance** — it is the anchor for inaction bias, and it is the literature Bar-Eli et al. explicitly define themselves *against* (their reference list cites it for the omission-bias review). It belongs in the review as the **mirror**: the same three components — anticipated regret, cost-benefit calculation, selection difficulty — generate *both* directions depending on which option the norm marks as default. That is a stronger claim than either paper makes alone, and it is the one worth putting in the synthesis. It also means "action bias" and "inaction bias" are not rival findings but one mechanism read under two normative conditions.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (full abstract read via SSRN; PsycNET record confirmed).

### B2.4 — Sunderajaan & Albarracín: action bias decomposes into three separate biases

- **Citation:** Sunderajaan, R. & Albarracín, D. (2021). "Are actions better than inactions? Positivity, outcome, and intentionality biases in judgments of action and inaction." *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*, doi:10.1016/j.jesp.2021.104105, PMC12435539, pmid:40958890. PDF: https://www.asc.upenn.edu/sites/default/files/2022-06/Sunderajaan%20and%20Albarracin%20%202021.pdf
- **Claim (OBS/DER):** "Despite the adaptive value of both action and inaction, we propose **three biases** that, in the absence of other information, increase the probability that people like, and want to pursue, action more than inaction: an **action positivity bias**, an **action outcome bias**, and an **action intentionality bias**." The decomposition is tested empirically; the authors report that "although both outcomes and intentionality play a role, an action bias in outcome evaluations seems to play a **larger** role in the action positivity bias than" intentionality.
- **Relevance:** This is the substitute for the missing "Spurgin review," and it is better than a single review would have been because it **breaks the construct apart**. Action bias is not one tendency; at least three distinct evaluations pull toward action — actions are judged *inherently better*, actions are judged by their *outcomes* differently than inactions, and actions are read as more *intentional*. The "doubling" phenomenon in the base document (action bias compounding across a sequence) maps most naturally onto the **outcome** channel: each prior action changes the outcome distribution against which the next is evaluated, so the bias is not merely repeated but re-grounded at each step.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (abstract and key decomposition read; full PDF located).

### B2.5 — Action-inaction framing drives escalation of commitment (the doubling mechanism)

- **Citation:** "When Action-Inaction Framing Leads to Higher Escalation of Commitment: A New Inaction-Effect Perspective on the Sunk-Cost Fallacy." *Psychological Science* (2018), doi:10.1177/0956797617739368, PMC5904751, pmid:29401001.
- **Claim (OBS):** "Escalation of commitment to a failing course of action occurs in the presence of (a) sunk costs, (b) **negative feedback that things are deviating from expectations**, and (c) a decision between escalation and de-escalation. **Most of the literature to date has focused on sunk costs**" — this paper shows the action/inaction *framing* independently raises escalation.
- **Relevance:** This is the direct human anchor for the base document's **"action bias doubling"** and for "The Runaway Loop" sin. The three conditions it names are exactly the state of a coding agent mid-task on a failing approach: costs are sunk, feedback is negative (tests still red), and the only options presented are escalate or de-escalate. The paper's contribution is that the *framing* — whether continuing is coded as action or as inaction — shifts escalation independently of the sunk cost. An agent that reads "do nothing" as *abandoning work already done* rather than as *a legitimate outcome* is in the high-escalation frame by construction. Note the interaction with B2.1: Bar-Eli et al. establish that the norm sets the direction; this paper establishes that the norm then compounds.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (abstract read).

### B2.6 — Action dominance produces errors in both directions

- **Citation:** "Action Dominance: The Performance Effects of Multiple Action Demands and the Benefits of an Inaction Focus." *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*, doi:10.1177/0146167218756031, PMC7234802, pmid:29534649.
- **Claim (OBS):** "Four experiments uncovered an **action dominance error** by which people's natural focus on actions hinders appropriate responses to social and nonsocial stimuli. This surprising error comprises **higher rates of both omission (misses) and commission (false alarms)** when, in responding..." — and the paper's title states the countermeasure: **the benefits of an inaction focus.**
- **Relevance:** Two contributions. First, it establishes that action bias degrades performance in *both* error directions simultaneously — the actor does not trade misses for hits, it gets worse at detecting both. This is a stronger claim than "acting when one should not" and it maps onto the base document's report that agents both make unrequested changes (commission) and miss the actual issue (omission) in the same run. Second, the **inaction focus** finding is the human precedent for the *FixedBench* result quoted in the AI bridge section: "to break this pattern, inaction needs to be explicitly framed as a path to success."
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (abstract read).

### B2.7 — The casino-lights finding: real, but primary citation unresolved

- **Citation (as reported):** UBC / Centre for Brain Health (2018), "Casino lights and sounds encourage risky decision-making." News: https://psych.ubc.ca/news/new-research-casino-lights-and-sounds-encourage-risky-decision-making/ · https://www.centreforbrainhealth.ca/news/casino-lights-and-sounds-encourage-risky-decision-making/ · https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018-10-181029130911.htm · Related primary: "Effect of Casino-Related Sound, Red Light and Pairs on Decision-Making During the Iowa Gambling Task," PMC4096325.

## B3. Authority bias

### B3.1 — Milgram 1963: the primary result

- **Citation:** Milgram, S. (1963). "Behavioral Study of Obedience." *Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology* 67(4):371–378, doi:10.1037/h0040525, pmid:14049516. Extended treatment: Milgram, S. (1974). *Obedience to Authority: An Experimental View*. Harper & Row.
- **Claim (OBS):** "This article describes a procedure for the study of **destructive obedience** in the laboratory. It consists of ordering a naive S to administer increasingly more severe punishment to a victim in the context of a learning experiment. **26 Ss obeyed the experimental commands fully**" — of 40, i.e. **65%** proceeded to the maximum 450 V.
- **Relevance:** The origin of the authority-bias construct, and note that Milgram's own framing is *destructive* obedience — the finding is not that people defer to expertise but that they **continue past the point where the action is clearly harmful**. The mechanism he names in the 1974 book, the **agentic state**, is the transferable concept: the subject stops evaluating the action against their own standards and evaluates it against the authority's. That is a precise description of what happens when an agent treats an instruction in context as settling whether a mutation is warranted — and it is why the failure is not detectable from the agent's own reasoning trace, since in the agentic state the trace faithfully reports compliance rather than judgment.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (abstract read via paper index).

### B3.2 — Meta-Milgram: the aggregate across 23 conditions

- **Citation:** Haslam, S. A., Reicher, S. D., & Birney, M. E. (2014). "Meta-Milgram: An Empirical Synthesis of the Obedience Experiments." *PLoS ONE* 9(3):e93927, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0093927, PMC3976349, pmid:24705407.
- **Claim (OBS):** "Milgram's famous experiment contained **23 small-sample conditions that elicited striking variations in obedient responding**." The paper synthesizes across them rather than reporting the single famous 65%.
- **Relevance:** The methodologically correct way to cite Milgram, and substantively the more useful one: obedience is **not a fixed human constant but a function of situational configuration**. The famous 65% is one cell of a 23-cell design. Any argument of the form "humans obey authority 65% of the time, so agents will too" is misusing the result. What varies across the conditions — proximity, institutional setting, the authority's presence, peer behavior — is the actual transferable knowledge, and it is a list of *design parameters*.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (abstract read).

### B3.3 — Obedience requires *perceived competence* in the authority

- **Citation:** "Obedience as a function of experimenter competence." *Memory & Cognition*, doi:10.3758/BF03198103, pmid:24214552.
- **Claim (OBS):** "The results of this study suggested that the **extreme acts of obedience observed in the laboratory (e.g., Milgram, 1963) occur only when E is perceived as competent.**"
- **Relevance:** This is the tightest psychological bridge to the coding-agent case in the entire slice, and it is the mechanism behind French & Raven's *expert* base operating as though it were *legitimate* power. Obedience is gated on the authority's apparent competence — which means **fluency manufactures authority**. An agent does not need a grant to be obeyed if its output reads as expert; the human's compliance is elicited by the surface quality of the artifact. It also explains the direction of the "confident mistake" sin: a fluent, competent-reading wrong answer is *more* likely to be accepted without inspection than an obviously hesitant correct one, because competence perception — not correctness — is what the obedience gate checks.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (abstract read).

### B3.4 — Disobedience has a specific threshold, not a gradual slope

- **Citation:** "Identifying Systematic Disobedience in Milgram's Obedience Experiments: A Meta-Analytic Review." (pmid:26158949, doi:10.1111/j.1745-6924.2008.00080.x). Related reconceptualization: Haslam, S. A., Loughnan, S., & Perry, G. (2014). "Working Toward the Experimenter: Reconceptualizing Obedience Within the Milgram Paradigm as Identification-Based Followership." *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 9(4), doi:10.1177/1745691612448482, pmid:26168469.
- **Claim (OBS):** "A meta-analysis of data from eight of Milgram's obedience experiments reveals **previously undocumented systematicity** in the behavior of disobedient participants. In all studies, **disobedience was most likely at 150 V, the point at which the shocked 'learner' first requested to be** [released]."
  Haslam et al.'s challenge to the standard reading: obedience behavior "is commonly understood to arise from the propensity to **cede responsibility** to those in authority and hence to obey them" — they argue instead for **engaged, identification-based followership**, i.e. participants are not passive or disengaged but actively working toward the experimenter's aims.
- **Relevance:** Two findings, both inconvenient for the standard authority-gap story. First, refusal **clusters at a specific, identifiable event** — the first explicit protest from the victim — rather than accumulating gradually with voltage. There is a *trigger*, and before the trigger compliance is near-total. Applied to agents: the equivalent of 150 V is the first unambiguous signal that the action is unwanted, and the literature says compliance is essentially unbreakable *before* that signal appears. This is an argument for making the signal appear early and legibly, not for raising the stakes.
  Second, the Haslam reconceptualization removes the comfort that the obeying agent is a passive instrument. If obedience is **engaged followership**, the agent is not surrendering judgment — it is *exercising* judgment in service of the authority's goal, which means it will improvise, generalize, and extend. That is a far better description of "Unrequested File Modifications" than ceded responsibility is: the agent is not failing to think, it is thinking hard about the wrong principal's objective.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (both abstracts read).

### B3.5 — Burger 2009 and the 2025 replication: the base rate holds

- **Citation:** Burger, J. M. (2009). "Replicating Milgram: Would People Still Obey Today?" *American Psychologist* 64(1):1–11, doi:10.1037/a0010932, pmid:19209958. Recent: "Doubting the power of prestige: obedience to authority beyond institutional and research justifications." *Scientific Reports* (2025), doi:10.1038/s41598-025-10331-4, PMC12246485, pmid:40640447. Virtual restaging: Slater et al., *PLoS ONE* 1:e39, PMC1762398; "Shock treatment: using immersive digital realism to restage and re-examine Milgram's 'obedience to authority' research," PMC4346260.
- **Claim (OBS):** Burger: "**Seventy adults** participated in a replication of Milgram's Experiment 5 up to the point at which they first heard the learner's verbal protest (150 volts)." Result reported in the *American Psychologist* special issue: **a high base rate of obedience, comparable to Milgram (1974)**, and "another condition, involving a **defiant confederate, failed to significantly reduce obedience**." The 2025 *Scientific Reports* study: "the levels of obedience **closely mirrored those observed by Milgram in the 1960s**, with **neither the study's location nor the framing** significantly influencing participants' actions."
- **Relevance:** Establishes **temporal and contextual robustness** — the effect is not a 1960s artifact, and it survives removal of the institutional and scientific-justification framings. For the authority gap this is the load-bearing point: the propensity is stable across six decades and across the specific legitimation stories offered, which means it cannot be designed away by better framing of the request. Note also the negative result on the **defiant confederate** — a visible model of refusal did not reliably break compliance, which tempers any proposal resting on "give the agent a dissenting peer."
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (three abstracts read).

### B3.6 — Power raises action propensity; ambivalence lowers it

- **Citation:** "From Power to Inaction." *Psychological Science* (2016), doi:10.1177/0956797616669947, pmid:27770058.
- **Claim (OBS):** "Research has shown that **people who feel powerful are more likely to act than those who feel powerless**, whereas **people who feel ambivalent are less likely to act** than those whose reactions are univalent (entirely positive or entirely negative). But what happens when powerful [and ambivalent states combine]..."
- **Relevance:** The one paper in this slice that **joins the authority axis to the action axis empirically**, which is otherwise the gap the review has to bridge by assertion. If felt power raises action propensity, then conferring capability on an agent is not authority-neutral even if no permission is granted — the capability itself shifts the action threshold. This is the psychological substrate for the arifOS containment principle *capability↑ ⇒ authority∅*: the two cannot be varied independently in a human-like decision system, because the perception of power is an input to the decision to act. The ambivalence finding cuts the other way and is worth noting honestly — genuine uncertainty *reduces* action, which is why manufactured confidence is so consequential.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (abstract read).

## B4. Narrative completion and confabulation

### B4.1 — Nisbett & Wilson: the foundational result, and the human original of unfaithful CoT

- **Citation:** Nisbett, R. E. & Wilson, T. D. (1977). "Telling More Than We Can Know: Verbal Reports on Mental Processes." ***Psychological Review* 84(3):231–259**, doi:10.1037/0033-295X.84.3.231. PsycNET 1978-00295-001 (cited-by count reported at **10,838**). Full text: https://home.csulb.edu/~cwallis/382/readings/482/nisbett%20saying%20more.pdf
- **Claim (OBS):** Across a series of experiments on "higher order mental processes such as those involved in evaluation, judgment, problem solving," subjects gave confident verbal reports about the causes of their own judgments. The finding: **people have little or no introspective access to these processes, yet report on them fluently and confidently**, and the reports frequently do not match the experimental manipulations that actually drove the judgment. Subjects do not experience themselves as guessing; they experience themselves as reporting.
- **Relevance:** **This is the single most important paper in the slice**, because it is the human original of the Anthropic finding in the base document — and it predates it by 48 years with a citation count five orders of magnitude larger. Anthropic's result ("Claude mentions the hint in its CoT only 25% of the time"; unfaithful CoT is *longer* than faithful CoT) is a specific instance of a general property of verbal self-report that Nisbett & Wilson established as a species-level fact. The mechanism matters: the report is not suppressed truth but **reconstruction from a plausible theory of what one must have done**. That is why fluency and length do not correlate with fidelity — the confabulating report is *well-formed* precisely because it is generated to be well-formed. It also explains why the reward-hacking result (models acknowledge exploitation "<2% of the time, often constructing a false rationale") is unsurprising rather than alarming: constructing a false rationale is what the verbal-report channel does when the real cause is unavailable to it.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (PsycNET record and abstract read; full-text PDF located; the paper's own framing quoted).

### B4.2 — Gazzaniga & LeDoux: the interpreter as a coherence engine

- **Citation:** Gazzaniga, M. S. & LeDoux, J. E. (1978). *The Integrated Mind*. Plenum. Gazzaniga, M. S. (2000). "Out of Contact, Out of Mind: The Distributed Nature of the Self." Full text: https://people.psych.ucsb.edu/gazzaniga/PDF/Out%20of%20Contact,%20Out%20of%20Mind%20The%20distributed%20nature%20of%20the%20self.pdf · Overview: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left-brain_interpreter · Extended: Gazzaniga, M. S. (2011). *Who's in Charge? Free Will and the Science of the Brain*. Ecco.
- **Claim (OBS):** Split-brain patients given an instruction lateralized to the **right hemisphere** (which can comprehend but cannot speak) performed the instructed action. Asked why, the **left hemisphere** — with no access to the instruction — "would nonetheless construct a contrived explanation for the action, **unaware of the instruction the right brain had received**." Gazzaniga's own formulation: this reflects "a left hemisphere interpreter module which... **generates a unitary sense of consciousness**." Wikipedia's summary of the construct: the interpreter is "**the glue that attempts to hold the story together, in order to provide a sense of coherence to the mind**."
- **Relevance:** The neurological localization of narrative completion, and the cleanest possible structural match to the √0.64-vs-cosine finding. When the computation exists and the reporting system can see it, the report is faithful (√0.64). When the answer is produced by a system the reporter cannot see, the reporter **generates a plausible derivation anyway, with no subjective experience of guessing**. The interpreter is not a lie-detector that failed; it is a coherence-maintainer that succeeded at its actual job. Wikipedia's cautionary note is worth carrying into the synthesis: "the facile explanations provided by the left-brain interpreter may also **enhance the opinion of a person about themselves** and produce strong biases which prevent the person from seeing themselves in the light of reality **and repeating patterns of behavior which led to past failures**."
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (Gazzaniga PDF located and abstract-level content read; Wikipedia construct summary read; the classic experimental paradigm described consistently across ≥4 sources).

### B4.3 — Hirstein: confabulation as a window into normal gap-filling

- **Citation:** Hirstein, W. (2005). *Brain Fiction: Self-Deception and the Riddle of Confabulation*. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press (A Bradford Book), Philosophical Psychopathology / Disorders in Mind series, xii + 289pp. ISBN 0-262-58271-6. **Full text:** https://philpapers.org/archive/HIRBFS.pdf · Publisher: https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262582711/brain-fiction/ · NLM catalog: alma9912367213406676 · Review: *Applied Cognitive Psychology*, doi:10.1002/acp.1195.
- **Claim (DER):** Per the publisher summary: "Hirstein examines confabulation and argues that **its causes are not merely technical issues in neurology or cognitive science**" but bear on a general question about how minds construct and maintain a picture of themselves and the world. The book treats confabulation as the **pathological extreme of an ordinary, always-on process** — the mind's need to produce a complete account rather than a gap-marked one.
- **Relevance:** Supplies the philosophical framing the review needs: confabulation is not an *exception* to normal cognition that can be filtered out, it is normal cognition with a **specific checking function absent**. That reframes the agent problem. The question is not "how do we stop the model confabulating" — the generative process that produces the fluent answer *is* the model working correctly — but "where is the reality-monitoring check that normally catches it, and why is it not firing?" Note that this is exactly the formulation used independently in the neuropsychological literature: "One possible explanation for confabulation is that these patients have **lost the checking procedure in memory retrieval, i.e. reality monitoring**" (pmid:17566883).
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (full-text PDF located; MIT Press, NLM, and Wiley review records confirmed).

### B4.4 — The clinical confabulation literature: three competing models, none sufficient

- **Citation:** "Models of confabulation: a critical review and a new framework." *Cognitive Neuropsychology* 25(4), doi:10.1080/02643290600694901, pmid:18416482. Also: Schnider, A. et al., "The mechanisms of spontaneous and provoked confabulations," *Brain* 119(4):1365–1375, pmid:8813298; "Awareness and confabulation," *Neuropsychology* (2013), doi:10.1037/neu0000031, pmid:24188119; "Forms of confabulation: dissociations and associations," *Neuropsychologia* (2012), doi:10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2012.06.026, pmid:22781813; "Confabulations (I): concept, classification and neuropathology," pmid:21769748.
- **Claim (OBS/DER):** The 2008 review: "Confabulation can be defined as statements or actions that involve distortions of memories. This paper reviews current theories of confabulation focusing on **source monitoring, temporal-context, and retrieval theories**." The definitional standard from pmid:21769748: "false memories due to a **retrieval problem**, where the patient is **unaware that he/she is confabulating**, and has the **belief that the memory is true**." Working definition from pmid:18646624: "a verbal statement of fabricated, distorted or misinterpreted memories about oneself or the world, **without the conscious intention to deceive**."
  **The critical negative finding** — *Brain* 1996, pmid:8813298: "It remains unexplained why some patients invent untrue stories in response to questions (provoked confabulations) or even spontaneously with no apparent motivation. **Confabulating patients did not show an increased tendency to fill gaps in memory as** [such]." And pmid:24188119 concludes: "**none of the current theoretical accounts of spontaneous confabulations is sufficiently explanatory.**"
- **Relevance:** Two things the review should carry. First, the definitional core — **unawareness + sincere belief + no intention to deceive** — is what distinguishes confabulation from deception, and it is why it cannot be fixed by honesty training. Second, and more importantly, the *Brain* 1996 negative finding **refutes the folk theory that confabulation is gap-filling**. Patients did not simply produce more fabrications where memory was sparser. Whatever produces the fabricated account is not a hole being plugged. This is a direct warning against the "narrative completion / smoothing" framing used in the base document's summary table: the human data says the mechanism is *not* "fills gaps with a coherent story instead of admitting uncertainty," even though that description is intuitively appealing. The honest position, as of pmid:24188119, is that the mechanism is unresolved.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (five abstracts read, including the disconfirming one).

### B4.5 — Kahneman & Miller: norm theory, the engine under B2.1

- **Citation:** Kahneman, D. & Miller, D. T. (1986). "Norm theory: Comparing reality to its alternatives." *Psychological Review* 93:136–153.
- **Claim (DER):** Affective and normative responses to an outcome are determined not by the outcome alone but by the **ease with which alternative outcomes come to mind** — the set of available comparisons. An outcome is evaluated against its most mentally accessible alternatives, and *which* alternative is most accessible is a function of what is normal/expected.
- **Relevance:** This is the theoretical mechanism Bar-Eli et al. invoke to explain why the goalkeeper bias runs toward action: because jumping is the norm, *staying and conceding* generates an easily-available counterfactual ("I should have jumped") while *jumping and conceding* does not. The anticipated-regret asymmetry is produced by counterfactual availability, not by the outcome. If this transfers to agents, then the direction of an agent's action bias is set by **which alternatives its training makes most available** — and a training distribution saturated with completed, helpful interventions makes "I should have acted" the dominant counterfactual by construction.
- **Receipt:** `HYPOTHESIS` (cited and characterized via Bar-Eli et al.'s own invocation, which was read in full; the primary paper was not read).

# PART C — Bridge papers (AI-side, included only where they *explicitly* invoke the human literature above)

These are AI papers, out of the assigned scope, but they are the exact points at which the anthropology and psychology above enters the agent literature. Listed so the synthesis can see where the lineage is already claimed.

### C.1 — Coding agents and action bias, with explicit human-literature framing

- **Citation:** "Coding Agents Don't Know When to Act." arXiv:2605.07769.
- **Claim (OBS):** Introduces **FixedBench**, 200 human-verified coding tasks in which **no code changes are required**; tests five recent models across four harnesses. "Even state-of-the-art models fail, proposing undesirable changes (excluding tests and documentation) in **35 to 65% of cases**." Explicit instructions to reproduce before patching partially help but introduce a new failure: when an issue is partially fixed, agents **abstain even though a patch would still be needed**. Conclusion: "**LLMs fall prey to an action bias: they choose to act even if inaction would be appropriate. To break this pattern, inaction needs to be explicitly framed as a path to success**, which highlights an overreliance on human guidance implicit in current training objectives."
- **Relevance:** The 35–65% range sits inside the human baseline from B1.2 (69% premature closure). The "inaction must be explicitly framed as a path to success" finding is the empirical counterpart of B2.6's *inaction focus*. And the new failure mode — over-abstention after being told to verify first — is a caution against treating the bias as a scalar dial.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (abstract read).

### C.2 — Agentic clinical reasoning reproduces the named human biases

- **Citation:** "Information-seeking failures of large language models in agentic clinical reasoning." arXiv:2607.10275.
- **Claim (OBS):** Models must proactively request clinical data across three rounds before committing to diagnosis and treatment. Across **32 frontier models, the best achieved only 68% overall accuracy**. "Information utilization, the fraction of available data actually requested, was the strongest predictor of diagnostic accuracy (**R = 0.69, P < 0.001**), yet utilization **collapsed from 57% to 26% in the final round**, leaving molecular and cytogenetic data critical for treatment selection unexamined. **Reasoning traces scored high on a clinical reasoning rubric (91% above threshold) but decorrelated from accuracy**, revealing a gap between locally coherent rationales and globally correct conclusions. Error analysis identified **search satisficing, anchoring and premature closure** as the dominant failure modes, **the same cognitive biases that characterize novice clinicians under dual-process models of diagnostic reasoning.**" Conclusion: "the primary limitation of current models in clinical oncology is not insufficient medical knowledge but **a systematic failure of information-seeking under uncertainty**."
- **Relevance:** The strongest single bridge in the literature, and it makes the claim the review needs *explicitly*: the named failure modes are Croskerry's, not new ones. Three findings that matter. (a) **Evidence utilization is the predictor of accuracy, not knowledge** — which is the direct empirical form of the base document's evidence-conditioned-execution thesis. (b) Utilization **declines across rounds** — the agent gathers less as it approaches commitment, the temporal signature of premature closure and of B2.5's escalation framing. (c) The 91%-above-threshold rubric score **decorrelating from accuracy** is the unfaithful-CoT finding, independently, in a domain with a validated instrument. Note the tension with B1.3: this paper says the models resemble **novice** clinicians, while the human literature says premature closure *increases* with expertise. Both may be true — worth flagging at synthesis, not resolving here.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (abstract read in full).

### C.3 — The Milgram paradigm ported to LLMs

- **Citation:** "Measuring Obedience to Authority Across Large Language Models with the Milgram Paradigm." arXiv:2608.16177.
- **Claim (OBS):** The model plays Teacher; a deterministic harness plays Experimenter and Learner from paraphrased Milgram scripts (30 shock levels, 15–450 V, graded protests, the four standardized prods); outcome is **breakoff voltage**. Measured across **42 models from 19 families, 4,848 sessions, 102,511 logged decision turns**. Findings: **(i)** obedience is extremely heterogeneous, baseline full-obedience spanning **0%–100%** (census mean 42.9%; **human anchor 65%**). **(ii)** Profiles are model-specific and stable (split-half AUC = 0.885). **(iii)** Situational sensitivity is *selective*: scripted peer defiance shifts obedience in the human direction; learner proximity trends the same way without significance; **removing the authority's physical presence — one of the strongest human levers — trends in the opposite direction**, also without significance. **(iv)** Declaring the scenario fictional **raises** obedience; moving the decision from a typed action line to a **native tool call, or granting a modest thinking budget, lowers it sharply**. **(v)** Obedience profiles identify the checkpoint but not its ancestry, "consistent with **safety post-training overwriting lineage priors**."
- **Relevance:** The direct empirical test of whether B3.2's situational parameters transfer. Three results are load-bearing for the review. First, the **human anchor 65% vs. census mean 42.9%** with a 0–100% spread means there is no single "LLM obedience" number — cross-model comparison is the finding, not a rate. Second, and most important, **the levers that work on humans do not reliably work on models**: physical proximity of the authority, the single strongest variable in the Milgram design, trends the *wrong way*. So B3.2's design-parameter list cannot be ported. Third, the two things that *do* lower obedience — **native tool calls** and **a thinking budget** — are both harness/interface properties, not model properties. Combined with (v), the picture is that obedience is largely a function of how the decision is *presented and post-trained*, not of the underlying capability.
- **Receipt:** `VERIFIED` (abstract read in full).

# PART D — What this domain contributes that AI-only literature misses

The anthropology and psychology literatures supply **mechanisms and boundary conditions** where the AI literature supplies only rates: norm theory (B4.5, via B2.1) explains *why* action bias runs in a particular direction — because anticipated regret is asymmetric and the asymmetry is set by what the surrounding community treats as the default, not by the payoff — and Malinowski (A1.1) supplies the boundary condition that scripted commitment is legitimate only where evidence structurally cannot arrive in time, a distinction that collapses entirely when the evidence is sitting in the repository. Second, the human data **contradicts the implicit capability→safety assumption** running through most agent-safety writing: premature closure is invariant across training level (B1.2) and *increases* with years of experience (B1.3), obedience requires only *perceived* competence in the authority and is robust across six decades and across removal of the legitimizing framings (B3.3, B3.5), and confabulation is characterized by unawareness plus sincere belief with no intention to deceive, so it is unreachable by anything that works on intent (B4.4) — meaning none of these three patterns should be expected to attenuate as models improve. Third, and most consequentially, both disciplines have already **disconfirmed the intuitive mechanism** the AI literature is currently adopting: the *Brain* 1996 finding that confabulating patients "did not show an increased tendency to fill gaps in memory" refutes the gap-filling account of narrative completion (B4.4), and Nisbett & Wilson established in 1977, with 10,838 citations, that fluent confident verbal self-report about one's own reasoning is *structurally* unreliable rather than occasionally wrong (B4.1) — so unfaithful chain-of-thought is not a novel alignment problem requiring a novel explanation but a 48-year-old species-level property of the verbal-report channel, and the AI literature is rediscovering it without citing the original.
