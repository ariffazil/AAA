3. THE THREE FINDINGS THAT CHANGE THE DECISION

Finding 1 — the hull is undersized, and the yard is already fixed. FPSO Excalibur: built 2007, Sevan-type cylindrical, 70×70×27 m, 300,000 bbl storage, originally ~30,000 bopd on Chestnut [M: DNeX Bursa filing 20 Aug 2026; Sevan press]. Limbayong design needs 40,000 bopd and 150 mmscfd. That is a 31% oil shortfall, 50% storage shortfall, and no gas handling — Chestnut was oil-only. Delivery is contractually fixed at MMHE Pasir Gudang by 5 Nov 2026. MISC bought the form, not the capacity. Conversion estimated US$600M (range $475–890M).

Finding 2 — this is a gas field with an oil rim, not an oil field with a gas cap. Section 1 derives gas GRV at 1.86× the oil-leg GRV; most of the 790.9 bcf is non-associated. Section 7 quantifies the consequence: oil-only NPV −$29M / 9.7% IRR; gas adds $317M — 110% of total NPV. And there is no public gas sales agreement and no defined export route, with a ~140–180 km subsea tie to SSGP Kimanis the only credible path.

Finding 3 — 2029 first oil is not credible. Section 6 gives Q4 2030 as P50 (51 months from FID). Kikeh took 43 months, Gumusut-Kakap 44, Malikai 46 — all clean newbuilds. A used cylindrical hull converting in a yard with no cylindrical-conversion reference beating all three by 7–10 months is not a schedule.

7. THE ACTION

HOLD irrevocable capex. Open a six-month assurance window. Gate 1 on 1 April 2027.

Three deliverables by that date, total ~US$60M:

  1. Independent cost assurance against the $4.05bn WBS. If real capex > $3.5bn → NO-GO or re-scope.
  2. Conversion scope to lump-sum at MMHE, including gas handling and the 40 kbopd topsides. An open-ended conversion on a used hull is a cost-overrun generator.
  3. Gas sales agreement, or a written decision that the gas is stranded and a re-run of economics without it. This is US$317M, not a rounding error.

If all three land favourably, GO with a full FDP. If any fails, the project is a stranded-hull write-down, not a development.

8. THE STRATEGIC CASE, STATED HONESTLY

No economic model in this study justifies Limbayong as a standalone oil project. But it may still be correct.

PETRONAS Carigali is 100% WI. It is the operator. It has never led a Malaysian deepwater development. A host facility at Limbayong is the only way to monetise Bestari and any future discovery in the L-B-P trend. A host built for one marginal field is a stranded asset; a host with a second customer is infrastructure.

That is a defensible reason to sanction. It is not the reason given publicly. The public framing is oil economics and a US$35M bargain. The real case is portfolio and capability. Both can be true — but a sovereign operator should say which one it is buying, because they carry different hurdle rates and different failure modes.

9. WHAT THIS IS NOT

No PETRONAS reservoir model, well proposal, AFE, FDP, or gas contract was available. Every cost is benchmarked; every reservoir figure is analogue-derived; fiscal terms are generic (1993 deepwater PSC vintage assumed). Section 8 sets out the US$170–240M assurance programme a real FDP would require against the 12-month institutional norm. Treat the verdict as a structure for a decision, and the numbers as bounds awaiting the operator’s own data.

HEADLINE

The operator has itself published the two numbers that decide this project: the trap is a 30 km × 2.5 km elongate toe-thrust ridge (aspect ratio 12:1) with “varying faults frequency, vertical intercalation of thin-bed, thick sand reservoir and lateral compartmentalization”, and IPA has graded it industry top-decile reservoir complexity — that is not an analyst’s opinion, it is the sanctioning basis in PETRONAS Carigali’s own SPE paper. My independent geometric rebuild lands at a P50 STOIIP of 517 MMbbl and, at a 26% recovery factor, 134 MMbbl recoverable, which reproduces the booked 139 MMbbl to within 4% — so the number is internally honest but sits exactly at P50, with a P90 downside of 51 MMbbl. The static case is not wrong; it is unhedged: a 12:1 faulted ridge drained by 10 wells cannot deliver P50 recovery without lateral continuity that the same paper says is impaired.

KEY JUDGEMENTS

  1. The trap is real and the trap is the problem. A 30 km × 2.5 km toe-thrust ridge is a proven NW Borneo trap family (Kikeh, Gumusut-Kakap, Malikai all sit in the same fold-thrust belt), so trap presence risk is low. Trap efficiency risk is extreme: at 12:1 aspect ratio, the drainage radius of a deviated deepwater well (~1–1.5 km) covers 3–5% of strike length per well. Ten wells at ~3 km spacing is the mathematical minimum for the footprint, and there is no spare well for the compartment that is always wrong in a faulted ridge.

  2. The 139 MMbbl is a P50 number wearing a P90 label. My geometric rebuild is independent of the operator’s model (public dimensions only) and lands at 134 MMbbl at P50, reproducing the booked figure. That is a consistency pass, not a validation. The sanction case is therefore calibrated to the median outcome, and the median outcome of a top-decile-complexity reservoir is, by construction, the outcome that does not surprise you — half the time it is worse. Downside is 51 MMbbl, i.e. 5.1 MMbbl/well, which cannot carry a US$100M+ well plus its share of a US$4bn facility.

  3. Compartmentalisation is not a risk to be modelled; it is stated as a fact in the sanctioning paper. “Lateral compartmentalization which impend effectiveness of well drainage and pressure maintenance” is PETRONAS Carigali’s own sentence. The mitigation described is modelling — “incorporated faults re-interpretation, refined grid resolution, and change of facies prediction, increasing the stratigraphic compartments”. The operator’s response to more compartments was to add more compartments to the model and then to “focus assessment on low realization case”. That is intellectually honest and operationally dangerous: it means the FDP is designed around a distribution it cannot yet measure.

  4. The oil rim and the gas are two different projects sharing one hull. The 790.9 bcf needs a gas GRV of ~4.46 km³ at 250 bar / 90 °C — 1.86× the rock volume of the oil leg. A gas cap that large above a 123 MMbbl oil rim would imply a column the seal would not hold; the volume arithmetic therefore says most of the booked gas is non-associated, in separate fault blocks or at Bestari. Consequence: gas deliverability (150 mmscfd design [M: GEM]) is a separate subsurface bet with separate compartments, and it is being tied back across the same 10-well subsea architecture. If gas is one accumulation and oil is another, there is no shared pressure support and no shared upside.

  5. Four appraisal wells in 24 years is the entire static database, and the model has not been refreshed since 2022. SPE 210549-MS (Sep 2022) states “the first phase of development is to start in Q1 2023”. The subsurface work published in 2022 was already FID-ready; the 2023–2026 delay was commercial and facilities (FPSO process cancelled Aug 2022, “commercially unviable” Mar 2023), not geological. On the public record, no new subsurface data has been acquired between the 2022 papers and the Aug 2026 FID. The sanction is being taken on a four-year-old static model with the same four wells. (Falsifier in next section.)

  6. Recovery factor 26% is the optimistic end of what this geology supports. Deepwater turbidite waterfloods globally run 25–40%; the NW Sabah analogue behaviour is the low end of that — Kikeh (1,330 m WD, same belt, same thin-bed/thick-sand problem) required re-drilling after early well failures and sand production, and needed dedicated high-angle thin-bed wells to chase bypassed oil (IPTC-17694-MS). Limbayong stacks more complexity onto that template. I would not underwrite 26% without a DST and a 4D baseline; 18–22% is the honest planning number for the low case.

  7. The narrow drilling margin is a static-model symptom, not just a drilling problem. SPE 210549-MS reports a window “less than 0.5 ppg even at the surface section”, wells drilled toward S_hmin, and MPD as mandatory. That is a stress-state statement: the reservoir is being depleted-adjacent or the ridge is critically stressed. Critically stressed faults in a compartmentalised ridge means faults that are both seals and leaks depending on production-induced stress change — the exact mechanism that killed Kikeh injectors through poroelastic effects (SPE 122018-MS). Injector count and injector life are therefore static-model outputs, not facilities assumptions.

  8. The 10-well / 270 MMboe configuration is a bet that the ridge is one connected tank. If it is (best case), 26% RF and 139 MMbbl is achievable and the project works. If it is five tanks — which the operator’s own “increasing the stratigraphic compartments” language implies — then either recovery falls to ~18% (51 MMbbl P90) or well count must rise by 50–100% at US$80–150M each. There is no version of this field where the static model does not set the well count, and no version where the well count is settled.

KEY JUDGEMENTS

KJ-1. The published “design capacity” is nameplate, not forecast — and it is arithmetically exact. 14.53 MMbbl/y ÷ 40,000 bopd = 363.25 days; 1,550.4 MMm³/y = 150.005 mmscfd. Falsified if: PCSB’s FDP shows a 40 kbopd average year at 92% uptime (it cannot — that requires 43.5 kbopd nameplate).

KJ-2. No production forecast for this field can hold 40 kbopd for more than ~9.5 years, because that is the entire oil reserve at 100% uptime and zero decline. 139 MMbbl ÷ (40,000 × 365) = 9.52 years. Any profile showing a decade-long plateau at nameplate is consuming 100% of the reserves during plateau and then stopping dead — physically impossible. Falsified if: reserves are restated upward by >40%.

KJ-3. The 30 kbopd as-built figure is NOT the binding constraint — the binding constraint is deck area and storage. The Chestnut-era process train (30 kbopd, 20 kbwpd) is being scrapped; S4 sizes 5,500–8,500 t of new topsides onto ~3,500 m² of usable cylindrical deck. 40 kbopd + 150 mmscfd + 75 kbwpd on that footprint is the real question, and it is a weight and area problem, not a throughput one. My base case derates to 35 kbopd partly for this reason. Falsified if: the conversion contract publishes a topsides weight < 5,500 t with a 40 kbopd / 150 mmscfd train.

KJ-4. Ten wells cannot do all three jobs. This is the central reservoir-engineering finding. Holding 35 kbopd requires 6 producers at 5,833 bopd; replacing voidage at Bo 1.25 requires 43,750 rb/d of injection, which needs 2 injectors at ~21,875 bwpd each. That is 8 wells. The remaining 2 must carry 790.9 bcf — 396 bcf per gas well, versus an analogue range of 100–300 bcf. Limbayong is therefore either (a) a 12–14 well development with a second drilling campaign, (b) a phased development where gas comes later on its own wells, or (c) booking 791 bcf on two wells that will not deliver it. The public “10 wells” number is a 2021 pre-FID figure and should not be used as the well count in the economics. Falsified if: the FDP well list shows ≥3 gas wells and ≥2 injectors inside the 10.

KJ-5. Deliverability is not the risk; drainage is. 5,833 bopd per producer against a PI of 10–40 stb/d/psi requires only 146–583 psi drawdown — routine for a frac-packed deepwater completion, and comfortably inside sand-control limits at PI ≥ 20. The wells will make the rate. What they will not do is drain 75 km² from six points on a 30 km ridge with faults every few kilometres, at 12.5 km² per producer and ~3.5 km equivalent spacing, with four appraisal penetrations in 24 years. SPE 210079 says this in the operator’s own words: lateral compartmentalisation “impend[s] effectiveness of well drainage and pressure maintenance.” Falsified if: interference testing or 4D seismic shows lateral continuity across the fault blocks.

KJ-6. The reserve density is the tell. 139 MMbbl over a 30 × 2.5 km structure is 1.85 MMbbl/km² gross. Kikeh (~400 MMbbl) runs roughly 5–10× that density across its productive fault blocks. Either only ~15–25 km² of the 75 km² is productive (pay is concentrated in a few fault blocks and the rest is thin-bed waste), or recovery is being booked at the low end. Both readings say the same thing: this is a sweet-spot development of a complex field, not a full-field development — which is precisely what “marginal” means in the paper’s title. Falsified if: the FDP maps >40 km² of net productive area.

KJ-7. Gas is sold, not flared, and not primarily re-injected — but it is only ~20% of the revenue. Zero routine flaring rules out 135 mmscfd to flare; PETRONAS ZEVC and the 2024 Sabah GSA point to monetisation. At $3/MMBtu domestic gas, 790.9 bcf is US$2.37bn undiscounted against US$9.73bn for the oil at $70 — gas is 49% of the boe but 20% of the money. Falsified if: a GSA at ≥$5/MMBtu (which would lift gas to ~29% of revenue and is worth +US$370M NPV per the CEO memo) or if the FDP adopts gas re-injection for pressure maintenance, in which case 791 bcf is not Phase 1 revenue at all and the project loses ~US$2.4bn of undiscounted gas sales.

KJ-8. There is a gas-export infrastructure gap that the disclosed contract scope does not cover. 790.9 bcf must reach either the Sabah grid (Kimanis/SSGP, ~140–180 km) or an LNG route. The disclosed awards are subsea iEPCI (TechnipFMC, US$75–250M) and a US$35M hull. No export pipeline, no gas-processing module, no GSA has been disclosed. Either the gas is stranded behind an unfunded 150 km pipeline, or it is re-injected, or the scope is undisclosed. S4 and S7 must price this; S5 must not book gas revenue before 2032 without a GSA. Falsified if: a pipeline EPCIC or GSA is published.

KJ-9. Uptime 92% is achievable but the field life is the bigger facility risk. A cylindrical hull has good motion behaviour (Sevan’s selling point) and the 92% base sits mid-range for North Sea FPSOs at 90–94%; a 19-year-old converted hull on a 30 km subsea tieback with a validated turndown limit justifies the 88% low case. Each 1% of uptime over the 5-year plateau is worth ~0.64 MMbbl. The harder issue: the economic limit lands around 2049, when the hull is 42 years old. A 23-year field life on a 2007-built redeployed cylinder implies either a life extension capex in the 2040s or a late-life host swap — neither is in the FID scope. Falsified if: the conversion includes a 25-year design-life certification.

KJ-10. About 16.6 MMboe (6.1%) of the booked reserves will not be produced commercially. Below ~4 kbopd the FPSO’s opex exceeds revenue; the 2049–2053 tail (4.5 MMbbl + 72.7 bcf) needs a low-cost late-life host to happen. S5 should carry 254 MMboe as the commercial case and treat the balance as contingent. Falsified if: opex falls below ~US$8/boe or a third-party tie-back shares the facility cost.

WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND

  1. A published FDP production profile. Any single operator document with year-by-year rates supersedes this entire section. My numbers are an envelope, not a prediction.
  2. The well list with well types. If the 10 wells are e.g. 7 producers + 3 gas and injection is deferred, my voidage argument collapses and plateau is credible; if they are 6 + 2 + 2, KJ-4 stands.
  3. A DST or RFT/MDT from Limbayong-2 or any appraisal well. A measured PI above 30 stb/d/psi with kh > 5,000 md-ft would move me from “drainage-limited” to “rate-limited” and lift the plateau case toward 40 kbopd.
  4. Interference or compartment-pressure data. Proof that the fault blocks are in communication would raise recovery per well and validate 6 producers for 139 MMbbl.
  5. A signed GSA with volume, price and start date. This converts KJ-7 from judgement to fact and is the single largest swing in the economics.
  6. Evidence of a strong water drive or gas-cap support that reduces injection need to <20 kbwpd — that would free a well slot for gas or oil and fix the KJ-4 arithmetic.
  7. Reserves restatement. If 2P is truly 139 MMbbl, the 9.5-year nameplate ceiling is unarguable. If the booked number is a conservative FID-case subset of a larger 800 MMboe trend resource, the plateau could run longer.

HEADLINE

Ten subsea development wells, drilled sequentially by one 6th/7th-generation drillship over 23.5 months (714 days), will cost US$762M P50 (range US$566M–1,025M) — US$67M per well P50 at a US$450,000/day rig rate. That is US$2.82/boe on 270 MMboe.

Three things dominate this section and none of them is the day rate:

  1. The wells are the appraisal. FID was taken on 28 Aug 2026 on the strength of four appraisal wells spread across a 30 km × 2.5 km structure — roughly one well per 7.5 km of strike on a field the operator’s own SPE paper calls “industry top decile reservoir complexity” with “possible different depositional model”. The 10-well programme is therefore carrying subsurface risk it was not designed to carry.
  2. The drilling margin is under 0.5 ppg and MPD is mandatory. This is stated by PCSB’s own well-planning paper (SPE 210549-MS): narrow margin “even at the surface section”, wells “highly deviated and in the direction of the minimum horizontal stress”, with stuck pipe, differential sticking and skin damage called out as the principal hazards. A benign-environment day-rate comparison understates this campaign’s NPT exposure.
  3. The 2026–2028 floater market is tight and consolidating. 2026 disclosed 7G drillship fixtures ran US$309,500–540,000/day, with leading-edge units clearing US$550,000/day; no drillship newbuilds of consequence have been ordered since 2014; and the announced Transocean–Valaris merger removes a competitor from the tender list. A 714-day campaign awarded into that market is a seller’s contract, not a buyer’s.

Contradiction flagged: SPE 210549-MS describes “the first phase of development” starting Q1 2023. The project instead reached FID in Aug 2026. The geomechanics model and well designs in that paper are therefore 3–4 years old and must be re-validated against any wells drilled since — this is an assumption-risk item, not a paperwork item.

WELL DESIGN CONCEPT

No Limbayong well schematic, casing point, mud weight or completion tally is public. Everything below is a concept-level design built from the two PCSB SPE papers and Sabah deepwater analogues, and is classed accordingly. The principal will recognise the shape; the numbers are placeholders for the drilling programme, not measurements.

Drilling unit interface. 21” marine riser, 15,000 psi BOP (a 20k stack is not justified — there is no public evidence of HPHT at Limbayong, and 20k units carry a US$75,000–125,000/day premium). MPD rotating control device and a continuous-circulation system are not optional given the <0.5 ppg window.

A contingency string (7-5/8” or 5-1/2”) must be carried in the AFE. With a sub-0.5 ppg window, one in three wells in analogous toe-thrust plays needs an unplanned string, and at 1,200 m water depth there is no room to improvise. Budget effect: +US$4–7M on the affected well, and +8–12 days.

Well trajectory. Highly deviated to near-horizontal, drilled along the minimum horizontal stress direction — PCSB’s own stated requirement, which is also the direction that maximises wellbore instability in shales. This is the single largest NPT driver in the programme.

Completion type. Horizontal subsea tree (TechnipFMC Subsea 2.0 configure-to-order, consistent with the Sept 2026 iEPCI), 5-1/2” 13Cr tubing, permanent downhole gauges, inflow control valves with zonal isolation packers. ICVs are not a luxury here: with lateral compartmentalisation across 30 km, the ability to shut off a watered-out or gassed-out compartment without a rig re-entry is worth more than the US$1.0–1.5M it costs per well.

Sand control. Open-hole gravel pack with shunt tubes (alternate-path) in the thick, high-permeability sands; standalone premium screens as the fallback in thin-bed intervals where drawdown can be held low. Frac-pack is technically defensible but has thin local experience in Sabah deepwater and should not be the base case. Budget US$4.2M/well for two gravel-packed intervals.

Artificial lift. Gas lift, full stop. ESPs in a 1,200 m subsea well imply a US$10–20M intervention per failure and a rig queue you do not control. Gas lift gas comes from FPSO compression (150 mmscfd gas-handling capacity exists). Four to six mandrels, orifice-valve design, plus a gas-lift optimisation study before first oil.

Well count split. Public sources say 10 wells; they do not say how many are producers versus injectors. Base case assumed here: 6 oil/gas producers, 3 water injectors (75,000 bwpd capacity available), 1 gas producer or disposal well [A]. Injectors are ~10% cheaper (no sand control in the same form, no gas lift). If the split is 8 producers / 2 injectors, add ~US$10–15M to the campaign.

Duration logic. First well 72 days: no offset experience on this specific well path, MPD system commissioning, and a full data-acquisition programme (VSP, MDT, full LWD suite). The 58-day tenth well assumes a genuine learning curve — PCSB has never operated a deepwater development campaign before (SPE 210549-MS: Limbayong is “the company’s first deepwater operated asset”), so a 14-day improvement across 10 wells is an optimistic-but-defensible 20% curve, not a guaranteed one.

Batch vs individual. Recommendation: do not batch. Batch drilling pays when wells are clustered on a template or a manifold centre and the rig can skid or move in hours. Limbayong is a 30 km-long, 2.5 km-wide structure with subsea wells spread along it; every “batch” move is a 5-day anchor-handling operation, which destroys the economic case. What should be batched is procurement and services, not hole sections: one casing order, one screens order, one MPD contract, one integrated logging contract, and a single set of standard well designs reused with minimum modification. That is where the 5% learning-curve saving (−US$37M) actually comes from.

Overlap with facilities. Wells 8–10 finish after the FPSO should be on station. Two of the last three wells should be drilled to TD and temporarily suspended pending hook-up, so that drilling completion does not gate first oil. This is a deliberate schedule buffer and should be written into the drilling programme, not discovered during execution.

Market reality, and why this is a schedule risk rather than a cost line:

  • Disclosed 2026 7G fixtures: US$309,500–540,000/day. Leading-edge units cleared US$550,000/day in early 2026, with some 20k-capable units reportedly above that. The 2025 weighted average was ~US$401,000/day and the market “bottomed around US$400k”. A 2028 start at US$450k is therefore a mid-market assumption, not a pessimistic one.
  • Utilisation bottomed in the low-to-mid 80% range in 2025 and is expected to recover through 2H26 into 2027. Low-to-mid 80s sounds loose; for 7G units capable of a 714-day Malaysian campaign it is effectively sold out, because the qualifying fleet is small and geographically sticky.
  • No drillship newbuilds of consequence since 2014. Reactivation costs US$75–125M per unit and takes 12–18 months. Supply cannot respond to a 2027 tender. The fleet through 2030 is what exists today.
  • Consolidation is removing counterparties. The announced Transocean–Valaris merger would create the largest high-specification floater fleet in the world. Fewer bidders, more pricing discipline.
  • PETRONAS is competing with itself. In 2026 it re-tendered a drillship for its own ultra-deepwater Indonesia wildcat and signed a one-well US$58M deal — both drawing on the same South-East Asia floater pool that Limbayong needs. Group-level rig demand is not coordinated with project-level need.
  • PETRONAS activity is rising, not flat. 91 wells to be drilled in 2026 versus 79 in 2025 (+15%), plus 70 P&A. Jackups and floaters compete for the same crews, the same MPD specialists and the same logging talent.

Practical consequence. A 714-day firm requirement is unattractive in a tight market — contractors prefer 3–5 year programmes with escalation. Expect one of three outcomes: (a) a rig at the top of the band (US$520–560k/d, +US$45–70M on the campaign), (b) a start delayed 6–9 months while a unit frees up (+US$60–90M in FPSO standby and escalation), or (c) a bundled multi-field Sabah deepwater tender (Limbayong + Belud + future tiebacks) that creates the duration contractors want. Option (c) is the only one that solves the problem rather than paying for it. Tender strategy should be a board-level decision, not a drilling-team decision.

KEY JUDGEMENTS

  1. US$762M P50 / US$67M per well is the number to carry into the FDP economics. Anything below US$600M assumes a rig rate the 2026 market no longer offers and a learning curve PCSB has not yet earned.
  2. MPD is a US$25.5M programme line, not a well-site option. 30 days × US$85k × 10 wells. Removing it to save money would be the single most expensive decision in this section, because the alternative is stuck pipe at US$620k/day of spread.
  3. Ten wells is thin for this structure. 270 MMboe across a faulted 30 km fairway with lateral compartmentalisation implies ~27 MMboe per well. That is achievable in good Sabah turbidites but leaves no margin for a dry development well. One failed well costs US$50M and permanently removes ~10% of the well stock.
  4. Do not batch. The structure geometry forbids it. Batch procurement instead, and standardise the well design so that well 10 is a repeat of well 4, not a new well.
  5. The rig is the schedule. Everything else in this project — FPSO conversion, SURF installation, subsea tie-in — can be recovered. A missing drillship cannot. Award by Q1 2027 or rebaseline first oil.
  6. The claimed 20–30% well-cost advantage should be discounted to ~10–15% in the base case. It was an internal integrated-concept claim made in 2022, before PCSB had operated a deepwater well as operator.

APPRAISAL AND DATA-ACQUISITION WELLS STILL REQUIRED

This is the uncomfortable part. FID has already been taken (28 Aug 2026), so the pre-FID appraisal window is closed. What follows is therefore not “pre-FID assurance” — it is the minimum data acquisition needed to keep the development from discovering its subsurface problems through failed producers.

Evidence of the gap: four appraisal wells since 2002 (SPE 210079-MS), on a 30 km × 2.5 km structure, with the operator’s own paper acknowledging “possible different depositional model and greater degree of complication”. Limbayong-1 (2002) found gas; Limbayong-2 (Nov 2013) found 136 m of oil-bearing sands. Four penetrations across 75 km² of faulted toe-thrust is exploration-scale density, not development-scale.

Recommended programme (2 firm + 1 optional), drilled from the development rig before the development wells:

# Well (descriptive, not official nomenclature) Purpose Duration Cost
A Eastern fault block appraisal / step-out Test the block with the least penetration; core + MDT + full logging; confirm sand continuity and fault seal 55–65 d US$52–60M
B Central block appraisal with DST Production test with build-up; establishes productivity index, skin, compartment connectivity and whether sand control is genuinely required 65–75 d US$60–70M
C (optional) Downdip/OWC well or interference test Oil-water contact, aquifer support, lateral connectivity between injector-producer pairs 50–60 d US$48–55M

Total: US$100–160M, or 13–21% of the drilling budget and ~2–3% of total project capex. That is cheap insurance against a US$50M dry development well and a FPSO that arrives to find its well stock under-delivering.

Non-well data acquisition still required (and often skipped): - Shallow hazard survey and geotechnical borehole at each of the 10 development locations — the 2021 Benthic CPT programme covered 700–1,040 m water depth at selected sites, not all 10 well paths [M: offshore-technology]. - VSP / checkshot in the first two development wells to tie the depth model. - Formation pressure while drilling (FPWD) and mud-log gas data on every well, integrated into the geomechanical model in near-real-time — the <0.5 ppg window cannot be managed with a pre-drill model alone. - A re-validated mechanical earth model. SPE 210549-MS’s model was built for a Q1 2023 campaign. Any well drilled since then has produced data that either confirms or destroys it, and nobody outside PCSB knows which.

Subject: Limbayong Deepwater Development (Block G, ~120 km offshore Sabah; centroid 5.888 N, 114.510 E; 900–1,200 m water depth; oil + non-associated gas; 10 deepwater wells tied back; FID 28 Aug 2026 per Upstream).

DATA CONFLICT TO SURFACE (per FACT_BASE hard-rule 6): The DNeX Bursa filing of 20 Aug 2026 — the primary, audited source — describes Excalibur as a 70 m × 70 m × 27 m cylindrical FPSO with 300,000 bbl design storage. Offshore Magazine (4 Aug 2022, trade press) reports 60 m diameter / 270,000 bbl / 25,000 bopd. I have used the Bursa values as primary [M] and reported the trade-press figures as an [A] lower bound because (a) the Bursa filing is signed and audited and (b) the 60 m / 270 k numbers cannot be reconciled with a 70 m × 70 m × 27 m box (circumference 220 m, beam 70 m is incompatible with 60 m diameter). Owner-supplied data trumps trade press in this section.

HEADLINE

The Excalibur is a proven, used Sevan-type cylindrical FPSO — the right geometric form for a harsh-environment Malaysian deepwater host but the wrong size: nameplate oil-processing capacity (~27,500 bopd) is ~31 % short of the 40,000 bopd the field needs, storage is ~50 % short of the 600,000 bbl Limbayong spec, and the hull has zero gas-handling / no water-injection upgrade for a 150 mmscfd gas + 75,000 bwpd WI duty. FID has nevertheless been taken, MISC is buying the hull, and the tow is contracted to MMHE Pasir Gudang (named in the MOA as the delivery quayside), so the concept is locked. Recommendation: PROCEED but rebid the processing topsides as a Step 2 fabrication to give a credible route to the 40 k / 150 mmscfd / 75 kbwd design duty, and route non-associated gas to the Sabah–Sarawak Gas Pipeline (SSGP) at Kimanis (already 500 km, 750 mmscfd class) via a ~120–150 km subsea tie from the FPSO. Conversion CAPEX US$300–500 M, 24–30 months from yard-cut to first-oil, AACE Class 5 (-30 %/+50 %).

CONVERSION SCOPE & COST

Yard: Malaysia Marine & Heavy Engineering (MMHE), Pasir Gudang, Johor. Named in the MOA as the delivery quayside (DNeX Bursa filing Appendix I). MMHE is the MISC in-house yard and has done every MISC FPSO conversion for two decades (FPSO Cendor, FPSO Ruby, FPSO Peris). No FID-grade alternative yard is on the table.

Scope (brownfield, 19-year-old hull):

  1. Hull integrity recertification — full class re-entry survey, hull UT 100 % near critical nodes, turret bearing / mooring chain / anchor leg replacement or recertification, blasting + re-coating. The hull was warm-stacked since ~2022, which is less corrosive than cold-stack but the mooring system has had no load cycle in 4+ years.
  2. Topside removal, redesign, rebuild, reinstall — Excalibur’s Chestnut-era topsides were ~3,000 t and designed for 30 k bopd / 20 k bwpd / zero gas. Limbayong needs 40 k bopd + 75 k bwpd + 150 mmscfd gas + 600 kbbl storage. All of the processing train, all of the gas train, and the bulk of the water-injection train is new. Practical scope: 5,500–8,500 t of new topsides, integrated onto the cylindrical deck (3,500 m² usable).
  3. Storage upgrade — 300 k bbl as-built vs 600 k bbl required. Cylindrical hulls have a fixed central-storage ring sized at build. You cannot add 300,000 bbl to a 300,000 bbl hull. This is the single most important engineering decision in the project and the Section recommendation must address it directly: either accept a 6-day cargo cycle (vs the 14-day cycle the design spec implies) or design the additional storage as a calm-barge shuttle alongside (capex +US$150–250 M, schedule +8–12 mo).
  4. Power generation — full gas-turbine train replacement, with waste-heat recovery if Tier-4 emissions. Hull was electric-drive when new (Sevan spec) but no published nameplate MW.
  5. Gas train — new build: slug catcher, multiphase pumps, gas conditioning, glycol regeneration, vent/flare, gas metering skid. The Sabah non-associated gas is CO₂-bearing and the project is marginal, so the gas train should be sized for 150 mmscfd at peak day, not design day.
  6. Water injection — new 75 kbwpd HP pumps, filters, deoxygenation, scaling control.
  7. Mooring — Internal turret was designed for North Sea metocean; Sabah monsoon and deepwater cyclones impose different loads (typhoon swells). Re-engineer the chain size and anchor pattern; expect all 9 legs to be replaced.
  8. Marine systems — helideck (likely upgrade to Sikorsky S-92 class), lifeboats to current SOLAS, dynamic positioning interface (if any), offloading system — either tandem off-take to a shuttle tanker (current Anaconda-style system was a Sevan feature) or new SOFEC CALM buoy (capex impact).
  9. Commissioning & sail-away — incl. 2-tow trip: Nigg → Pasir Gudang (heavy-lift vessel, ~30 days, weather window) and Pasir Gudang → Limbayong field (~10 days, wet tow, monsoon-sensitive).

Brownfield integrity risk — the elephant in the room:

  • The hull is 19 years old at sail-away and 38 years old at end-of-field-life if Limbayong produces 10 years. Class societies typically grant 5-year recertification cycles; 38 years is beyond the design life of the original fatigue analysis even though a Sevan cylinder sees lower cyclic loading than a monohull.
  • The vessel was warm-stacked (Nigg, Scotland) — better than cold-stack (no anchoring cycles, no mooring fatigue) but the topsides were never designed for tropical duty, and the gas train is brand-new. The classic Sevan integrity watch-outs are: (i) bilge-keel / column welds in way of the cylindrical hull-to-deck joint — re-inspect 100 %; (ii) turret bearing wear after 4 years static; (iii) riser balcony fatigue in the new configuration.
  • The cylindrical hull’s only integrity advantage over a tanker-conversion is no monolithic flare tower / no midship strength-rail modifications — the Sevan design already integrates everything inside the 70 m disk, so there is no heavy structural cutting.

Why the cylinder is the right call — the Sevan geometry concentrates topsides mass over the moonpool/turret, gives a low-motion hull (better uptime in Sabah’s monsoon and typhoon swells), and is the only FPSO form MISC owns in this size class. The fact that the hull is used is not a defect — it is the only way a marginal field gets an FPSO at all. Industry norm: 40–50 % of new FPSO projects in SE Asia / NW Australia in 2024–2026 are used-hull conversions (PaxOcean, MMHE, Keppel order books).

Why storage is a non-fatal gap — Limbayong’s 139 MMbbl oil at 40 k bopd is 9.5 years of plateau; a 6-day shuttle cycle = 110 shuttle calls over field life = ~US$1.1 B of shuttle-tanker capex+opex (at US$10 M/yr charter × 110/12 yr). A SOFEC buoy alongside would push storage to 600 k bbl but adds US$150–250 M and 8–12 months. Recommend: shuttle-tanker offtake at 6-day cycle for first 5 years; review SOFEC at 50 % field-life if oil price supports it.

GAS HANDLING & EVACUATION ROUTE

Spec: 150 mmscfd of non-associated gas at peak, ~54.7 bcf/yr at design (per FACT_BASE §VOLUMES). 790.9 bcf remaining gas reserves (FACT_BASE, GEM 2023) at 54.7 bcf/yr = 14.5 yr gas plateau, longer than the oil plateau.

Route recommendation: dedicated subsea gas export pipeline from the FPSO to the Sabah–Sarawak Gas Pipeline (SSGP) at the Kimanis landfall, ~140–180 km of 16″–20″ pipeline, with a pipeline-end-manifold (PLEM) tie-in. The SSGP has been operating since 2014, is 500 km Kimanis → Bintulu (MLNG), 750 mmscfd class, and currently has spare capacity given the Kebabangan / Kamunsu East decline curves.

Why not (a) re-inject all gas: reservoir pressure support is required (the wells are deepwater toe-thrust, compartmentalised, water-injection-supported per FACT_BASE), but a 150 mmscfd gas-handling train is already spec’d. Flaring would breach PETRONAS zero-routine-flare; reinjecting 100 % requires extra compression and reduces sales-gas NPV.

Why not (b) send to Bintulu LNG via SSGP directly: 500 km transit with multiple offtake points; capacity is shared with Petronas upstream gas (Kebabangan, Bintulu Clastic, others). The Limbayong volume (54.7 bcf/yr) is meaningful — ~7 % of SSGP throughput — so a dedicated tie-in + booking would be needed and a pipeline capacity agreement with PETRONAS Energy Malaysia.

Why not (c) re-inject and re-inject, no export: leaks 790 bcf of revenue; gas-to-power for Sabah domestic has been the policy since 2014 (Borneo Post 2013).

Decision: Build the export pipeline. Indicative capex for a 16″–18″, 150 km subsea line is US$250–400 M (subsea 7″ equiv benchmark ~US$1.5–2.0 M/km × 1.5 for 16–18″ × 150 km + PLEM + SSGP tie-in skid). This is not in the S4 conversion capex above; it belongs in SURF / midstream. The FPSO topsides need only the slug catcher, gas conditioning, and metering skid (already in the US$40–80 M gas-train line).

Evacuation route (offshore personnel): Crew change by helicopter to a Sabah onshore base (Kota Kinabalu or Labuan, ~120 km), S-92 or H175 class on helideck sized to current CAP 437. Emergency evacuation: lifeboats to a stand-by vessel, then to shore. The cylindrical hull has 360° access around the deck which materially improves evacuation geometry vs. a monohull FPSO with a bow turret.

KEY JUDGEMENTS

  1. The cylindrical form is correct; the size is short. Sevan geometry is the only credible answer for a marginal Sabah deepwater field; the project lives or dies on a credible Step-2 topsides rebid that closes the 40 k bopd / 150 mmscfd / 75 k bwpd gap.
  2. Storage gap is solvable with shuttle-tanker cadence, not a SOFEC buoy. 6-day cycle is operationally fine for 9.5 years; the SOFEC decision can be deferred to 50 % field-life.
  3. The hull age is the manageable risk, not the project-killer. 19 years at sail-away is within the range of class society recertification practice; the real risk is mooring-system redesign for Sabah metocean, not the cylinder itself.
  4. Gas export to SSGP-Kimanis is the gas-NPV unlock. Without the export pipeline, 790 bcf of reserves is stranded gas; with it, the gas NPV roughly equals the oil NPV at current Malaysian regulated prices.
  5. FID has been taken; the alternative analysis is for the next FDP, not this one. All three rejects above are correctly archived for the Bestari and L-B-P Phase 2 development plan.

WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND

  • If a credible bidder offers a newbuild 70 m Sevan-type cylinder at ≤ US$900 M all-in with ≤ 30 months to first oil, switch from conversion to newbuild (storage, gas, WI all spec-fit, no integrity compromise).
  • If SSGP capacity is fully booked when PETRONAS Energy Malaysia confirms (expected Q1 2027), the gas export NPV collapses and the project reverts to gas-reinjection + reduced oil plateau; the 40 k bopd design rate then requires a 150 mmscfd gas-injection compression train (capex +US$120–180 M) and the project slips below commercial threshold.
  • If the DNeX-attributed 60 m / 270 k bbl / 25 k bopd numbers turn out to be the post-2022 Avalon-deck-removal as-built state (rather than a trade-press error), the capacity gap widens to ~38 % on oil and the Step-2 rebid is no longer optional — it is the project.
  • If the independent ABS class survey at MMHE finds hull thickness below class-acceptable at the column-to-deck weld (the classic Sevan fatigue hot-spot), the hull is scrap and MISC must source a substitute cylinder (effectively ending the project as currently scoped).
  • If Malikai TLP topsides are decommissioned before 2030 (Shell exit announcements in 2025–2026 are tracked), then a tie-back to Malikai becomes technically feasible for Limbayong if the TLP can be re-topsides for 40 k bopd + 150 mmscfd — at ~US$600–900 M all-in, this becomes the lowest-cost alternative to the conversion and the recommendation must flip.

HEADLINE

The full 10-well Limbayong SURF is a ~US$0.68 bn scope (Class 4: US$0.48–1.02 bn), not a US$75–250 M scope. The 2026 TechnipFMC “significant” band is a booking classification, not a cost estimate, and cannot describe the full scope: it is 2.7× below even the best-case build-up at the top of the band. The 2021 “US$250–500 M” band, escalated to 2026 at 4.5%/yr, lands at US$623 M — within 9% of my bottom-up number. That convergence is the strongest signal in this section: the 2021 award was approximately correctly priced for the scope, and the 2026 award is either a partial call-off, a phased booking, or excludes trees. Any FDP economics built on “SURF ≈ US$250 M” understates subsea capex by roughly US$430 M.

Second headline: the architecture that fits this field is not the cheapest one. Three drill centres at 6.5–18 km, 900–1,200 m, waxy Sabah crude, and an operator commitment to tie back prospects at 18–30 km all point to multiplexed electro-hydraulic control, insulated flexible flowlines, lazy-wave flexible risers, and a single dynamic umbilical to a subsea distribution unit with spare capacity. Direct hydraulic is technically excluded at these offsets, not merely uneconomic.

FIELD LAYOUT & ARCHITECTURE

Layout concept [A]

No public field map, well trajectory, or discovery timeline exists for Limbayong. The layout below is an explicit assumption set, built to be internally consistent and to stress the cost model at a realistic step-out. It is not a claim about the actual field.

  • FPSO at field centroid [A]; spread-moored or turret configuration unresolved [U] — the FPSO is being sourced as the used cylindrical hull Excalibur (US$35 M, DNeX→MISC, Aug 2026), and riser interface design depends on whether that hull carries a disconnectable turret. This is the single largest unhedged interface risk in S5.
  • DC-1 (Limbayong Main): 4 wells, 4-slot manifold, 6.5 km.
  • DC-2 (Central): 3 wells, 4-slot manifold, 12.0 km.
  • DC-3 (North/Satellite): 3 wells, 4-slot manifold, 18.0 km.
  • One 2-slot WI manifold at DC-1 serving both injectors.
  • 2 spare manifold slots retained deliberately: PETRONAS stated the project “will pave the way for the development of the surrounding prospects within 18 to 30 km.” Spare slots and umbilical spare tubes are strategic optionality for that stated follow-on, not gold-plating.

Controls: multiplexed electro-hydraulic, not direct hydraulic [A]

Direct hydraulic is excluded on three independent grounds: 1. Offset. Direct hydraulic is practically limited to roughly 5 km and ~20 functions before response time and hydraulic lag become unacceptable. At 18 km with 10 wells it fails outright. 2. Function count. Each tree needs SCSSV, production master, wing, choke, annulus vent, crossover, plus sand/pressure/temperature monitoring. Ten wells × ~8 functions plus manifold valves exceeds any direct-hydraulic hose bundle that could be installed economically. 3. Diagnostics. No feedback path, no condition monitoring, no remote choke adjustment — unacceptable for a field whose operator has publicly flagged “top decile reservoir complexity” and marginal economics. Flexibility to re-choke individual wells without a vessel is worth more than the control-system saving.

Base case: dual redundant subsea electronics modules per manifold, 2 × 50% hydraulic supply, fibre-optic in the umbilical for distributed temperature/acoustic sensing on the 18 km production line (leak and slug detection). All-electric actuation is technically available on Subsea 2.0 and would cut umbilical diameter and cost by roughly a third, but has no PETRONAS track record in Malaysia; treat as a post-FID value-engineering option, not the base case. [A]

Flexible vs rigid flowlines

Base case: flexible, insulated, reel-layable for all production, WI and GI lines. Reasons: - 900–1,200 m and 18 km sits inside the comfortable flexible envelope; 8” ID at 10 ksi is standard product, not a development item. - Compliant risers follow directly from flexible flowlines — no flex-joint, no riser base manifold, no SLWR buoyancy modules. - Reel-lay installation is roughly 2–3× faster than J-lay at these lengths, and the Sabah weather window is the binding constraint. - Insulation (5-layer PP, 50–70 mm) integrates into the pipe wall; pipe-in-pipe would be needed to match U-values with rigid steel.

Rigid steel would only win if (a) the gas export line to shore proceeds at 120 km — a 12–16” rigid SLWR plus pipeline is a different procurement and vessel class — or (b) reservoir pressure/temperature exceeds flexible limits (unknown: no public BHT/BHP for Limbayong). Flag as a falsifier, not a base case.

Manifolds and jumpers

Three 4-slot production manifolds, 10 ksi, 1,200 m rated, with pigging loop and integrated sand detection. Fifteen jumpers: 10 tree-to-manifold, 3 manifold-to-flowline, 2 interconnect. All ROV-installable — no saturation diving anywhere in this development, which removes an entire cost and safety category and is a genuine advantage of the 900–1,200 m range.

Risers: lazy-wave flexible, not free-standing

  • 3 × 8” production, 3 × 6” WI/GI, all lazy-wave flexible, plus 1 dynamic umbilical.
  • Free-standing riser rejected. A free-standing hybrid riser needs a buoyancy can, a foundation, and a flexible jumper to the FPSO; at 1,200 m it costs roughly US$80–150 M per tower. Justified only where riser count is high, pressure is extreme, or the host has severe motion. Here it would add US$100 M+ to solve a problem flexible lazy-wave already solves.
  • Steel lazy-wave riser (SLWR) retained only for the deferred 120 km gas export route.

Umbilical

Single 1.4 km dynamic section from FPSO to a subsea distribution unit, then static trunk and infield legs — 24.9 km total. This is cheaper than three parallel dynamic umbilicals (one per drill centre) and concentrates the fatigue-critical section in one place. Spare electrical and hydraulic cores and two spare 1” tubes are included for the 18–30 km prospect tie-backs.

Most probable readings, with my weights:

Vessel market and availability risk

  • Day rates are elevated but softening at the low end. 2025 fixtures: 150 t AHC crane vessels often >US$60,000/day; 250 t AHC >US$80,000/day — at or beyond the previous peak. Short-term and spot fixtures varied almost four-fold top-to-bottom in 2025, so a 2029 campaign cannot be priced off today’s spot. [M]
  • Supply is arriving. ~30 firm newbuild 150/250 t AHC vessels are on order, >20% of the active fleet — a ratio comparable to the 2008–2015 build wave, mostly Chinese-built, mostly speculative and uncommitted. This should loosen 2028–29 availability but widen the rate spread by vessel age and specification. [M]
  • Top-tier concentration. The Subsea 7–Saipem merger (Saipem7), expected to close H2 2026, leaves three Tier-1 SURF contractors and a combined market leader. For a single 211-day campaign in a remote basin, that is a thin bench: two credible bidders, not four. [M for merger, A for consequence]
  • Malaysian OSV market is seen as stable-to-positive over 2026–28, with a leaner offshore cycle per PETRONAS Activity Outlook 2026–2028 — supportive for support vessels, but the flex-lay/reel-lay class is not an OSV and is priced globally. [M/A]
  • Weather. Sabah offshore is less monsoon-exposed than Sarawak, but 1,200 m operations are swell-sensitive. Assume ~200 operable days/year and a hard March–October campaign window; every 30 days of slippage costs roughly US$10 M in standby and pushes RFSU past the next window. [A]
  • The real vessel risk is not rate — it is sequence. A single-season campaign installs 61.5 km of flowline, 24.9 km of umbilical, 6 risers, 4 manifolds and 15 jumpers with one flex-lay vessel. There is no second-vessel redundancy. A 20-day mechanical failure in June 2029 costs a full year to first oil.

KEY JUDGEMENTS

  1. SURF is US$682 M (Class 4: US$478 M–1,024 M), or ~US$68 M/well. The disclosed 2026 band understates the scope by at least 2.7×. Any economic model using US$250 M is not conservative — it is wrong by roughly US$430 M, which on a 270 MMboe field is ~US$1.6/bbl of undeclared capex.
  2. The 2021 and 2026 awards are not two estimates of the same thing. 2021 was a scope-valued award; 2026 is a booking class. Treating them as a declining cost trend — “TechnipFMC cut the price in half” — is a category error and the most likely analytical trap in this FDP.
  3. Multiplexed E/H control is mandatory, not optional. Direct hydraulic cannot reach 18 km with 80+ functions. The cost of MUX (US$48 M, 7% of SURF) buys remote choke management on a field the operator itself calls top-decile complex — that is the highest-value 7% in the budget.
  4. Flexible lazy-wave everywhere; free-standing riser is not a candidate. FSHR would add US$100 M+ to solve a motion problem that compliant flexible risers already solve at 1,200 m with six risers.
  5. Three drill centres with two spare slots is the right architecture, and it is also the tie-back option premium. PETRONAS has publicly committed to the 18–30 km prospect radius. Sparing slots and umbilical cores now costs perhaps US$15–25 M; retrofitting a manifold position later costs a vessel campaign.
  6. The binding constraint is flexible pipe, not the vessel market. 2029 vessel availability looks better than 2025; 2028–29 extrusion slots do not. This inverts the usual SURF risk ranking and should drive the procurement plan.
  7. No diving anywhere. 900–1,200 m means all-ROV installation and intervention. This is a real cost and safety advantage over shallow-water Malaysian analogues and should be protected in the design (ROV-friendly torque tools, no diver-assisted connectors).
  8. The FPSO interface is the largest unhedged item in S5. Whether Excalibur carries a disconnectable turret determines riser count, dynamic umbilical length, and whether the field can be storm-disconnected. Until that is settled, the riser and umbilical numbers carry an additional ±15% of their own value.

Critical path: FPSO conversion (Feb 2027 → Aug 2029) → hook-up → commissioning → first oil Nov 2030. Near-critical: rig availability. If the drillship is not contracted by Q1 2027, first oil moves month-for-month into 2031 — the wells chain has only ~4 months of float against the floater.

Why 51 months and not 36. The three Malaysian deepwater benchmarks all beat this: Kikeh 43 months (FID 2004 → first oil Aug 2007), Gumusut-Kakap ~44 months (FID 2011 → Nov 2014), Malikai 46 months (FID Feb 2013 → Dec 2016). Every one of those was a newbuild or purpose-built floater with a clean hull. Limbayong is a 1990s-vintage used cylindrical hull, on a reservoir the operator itself calls top decile complexity, in a yard with no cylindrical-conversion reference. Pricing this at 36 months would be assuming Limbayong executes better than three projects that all executed well and still took 43–46.

This is what “marginal” means in the operator’s own SPE 210079 title. The project is not expensive per barrel in the ground. It is expensive per barrel per day.

Overrun statistics — deepwater. IPA’s 2012 industry study (published via SPE 153695 / OTC-Brazil 29804) found 78% of upstream projects in 2011 experienced either a cost overrun or a schedule slip [M]. EY’s 2014 analysis put 64% of multibillion-dollar, technically challenging oil and gas projects into overrun or delay [M, EY release], with the industry-wide exposure above US$500bn [M]. IPA’s consistent finding across vintages: deepwater and arctic megaprojects are the worst-performing category, with median cost growth in the +20–40% band and schedule slip of 12–24 months [A]. That is the honest read: the +50% case is not a stress case, it is the median historical outcome for this asset class.

KEY JUDGEMENTS

  1. US$4.05bn is the number, and the −30% side is barely reachable. Ten deepwater wells at US$95M each and a US$755M converted cylindrical FPSO are floor-bound. The credible range is really US$3.6–6.1bn; the −30% case (US$2.83bn) requires deferring the gas line and a clean hull survey and a soft rig market simultaneously.

  2. The subsea contract halving is the tell, and it is not good news. Same contractor, same Subsea 2.0 model, ceiling cut from US$250–500M to US$75–250M between 2021 and 2026. That is not efficiency — Subsea 2.0 pricing did not halve. It means less scope is being bought at FID. My SURF line (US$640M) is ~2.5–8× the announced contract band, and the gap has to be reconciled either as separate tree frame agreements, a separate installation EPCI, or scope that is not yet contracted. Any of those three answers raises, not lowers, the risk that SURF is under-budgeted at sanction.

  3. The FPSO is the cost and schedule problem, not the wells. A US$35M used cylindrical hull bought from a UK North Sea operator is a hull with an unknown steel-renewal liability, and a cylindrical conversion is a specialist job with essentially no Malaysian reference. I have carried 31 months and US$550M for conversion. A clean special survey could save US$80M and 4 months; a bad one adds US$200M and 9 months. This is a binary I cannot resolve from public data — mark it [U] and manage it.

  4. First oil Q4 2030, and the operator will say 2029. The 36-month case requires beating Kikeh, Gumusut-Kakap and Malikai — all clean-hull projects — by 7–10 months. It is not credible. Budget the cash flow on 2030 and treat 2029 as upside.

  5. The gas line is the swing scope item. US$300M for a ~120 km wet-gas export line is a third of the FPSO cost and is only justified if the 790 bcf is monetised at a price that clears the tariff. If gas is deferred, capex drops to ~US$3.75bn and capex/boe on liquids alone (139 MMbbl) rises to US$27/boe — which is where the project starts to look uninvestable. The gas is not a bonus; on this cost base it is what makes the barrel economics work.

  6. Full-cycle breakeven is roughly US$48–56/bbl [D] at US$4.05bn forward + US$570M sunk, 270 MMboe, 15-year life, 10% discount rate, US$4–6/mmbtu gas. That is a thin margin against a US$65–75 deck for a project with a +50% cost tail. The correct verdict from S6 alone is conditional GO only if the hull survey and the rig contract are both closed before the first major FPSO payment.

The three questions asked directly

What oil price makes this a 15% IRR? US$96/bbl Brent, flat, real, for the whole 2027–2045 path ($75 × 1.284). That is 28% above base and 7% above my high deck. In the plausible 2027–2035 range, Limbayong does not earn 15% at any price.

What reserves number makes it break even? 233 MMboe (119.7 MMbbl + 681 bcf) — 86% of the booked 270.8 MMboe. You can lose 14% of the reserves and still return 10%. For 15% IRR you need 325 MMboe, 120% of booked. The band between “still stands” (86%) and “works properly” (120%) is narrow, and the booked number is a 2023 regulatory estimate for a field with no public well data.

Is the gas monetisation material or cosmetic? Material and decisive — it is the project. Removing gas (and the $650M pipeline) leaves NPV10 at −$29M and IRR at 9.7%: oil alone does not clear a 10% hurdle. Gas adds $317M of NPV, 110% of the total. Every one of the following is true simultaneously: the FPSO is justified by liquids throughput, the value is justified by gas, and the gas only works because it can be sold into a Sabah market at $6.50/MMBtu rather than at an LNG netback struck after liquefaction, shipping and the 5% state sales tax. The gas price floor is $3.86/MMBtu — below that the project is NPV-negative. At $10.22/MMBtu it reaches a 15% IRR, but that is above the RM30/MMBtu (~$7.1) domestic cap and is therefore not achievable into the Malaysian market.

KEY JUDGEMENTS

  1. This is a 10%-hurdle project priced by a company that does not need 15%. NPV10 of $288M on $2.9B of capital is a 1.10× PV/capex ratio. For a listed IOC that is a destroyer of value against a 15% cost of capital. For PCSB — 100% WI, sovereign balance sheet, explicit Sabah gas mandate — 12.3% real clears. FID is therefore rational as policy, not as portfolio economics. Say that plainly to the CEO: this is a strategic gas supply decision with an oil co-product, not an oil development with a gas by-product.

  2. The project’s investability is manufactured by a tax incentive. Remove the 60% investment allowance under the PITA (Investment Allowances) Regulations 2013 and NPV10 falls to −$65M. $353M of the $288M NPV — more than all of it — is the fiscal incentive. That is a defensible policy choice (deepwater, >200 m, marginal field, national gas supply) but the FDP should not be presented as commercially robust without naming this. If MOF declines or trims the IA, the project is dead on a 10% hurdle.

  3. Reserve risk dominates everything, and the public data cannot bound it. −30% reserves → NPV −$384M. The field has a 2002 discovery, a 2014 oil confirmation, no public well data, no public seismic, and an operator paper whose title contains “Top Decile Reservoir Complexity” and “Marginal”. A 14% shortfall breaks even. For a toe-thrust, faulted, compartmentalised turbidite with a 10-well development plan, a 14% shortfall is not a tail risk — it is close to the base case for this play type. The single most valuable thing PCSB can do pre-first-oil is appraise, not accelerate.

  4. Capex intensity of $11.45/boe is the number that will be quoted against this project. That is roughly 1.7× Gumusut-Kakap (~$6,900/boe) and closing on Malikai (~$14,000/boe) — the two Sabah deepwater benchmarks that bracket “acceptable” and “cautionary tale”. UTC of $21.06/boe follows from it. The driver is only 270.8 MMboe of reserves against $2.9B of capital; a 30% reserve upgrade alone would cut capex intensity to $8.24/boe and lift IRR to 16.3%. UTC is the cleanest single metric for communicating why this field waited 24 years from discovery to FID — and why the 2021–2023 standalone newbuild concept was cancelled.

  5. The $35M FPSO is the best capital decision in the project and the least analysed. Buying a used cylindrical hull for 1.2% of total capex fundamentally changes the economics versus the 2021–2023 standalone newbuild concept that was cancelled as “commercially unviable”. But a used 1990s hull carries condition, life-extension and uptime risk that a Class 4 estimate cannot see. The $200M abandonment and $450M conversion are judgements, not quotes. If the hull needs $650M rather than $450M, that is roughly −$85M of NPV — 30% of the project’s margin.

  6. 45.6% total take is light for Malaysia and I do not fully believe it. Published Malaysia PSC splits typically show 60–70% government take. My 45.6% reflects DW terms (70%/80% cost recovery, 50% profit split, 10% royalty, no supplementary payment modelled) plus a 60% IA. If the actual Limbayong PSC carries a supplementary payment, a higher profit share to PETRONAS, or no investment allowance, contractor NPV falls by a further $200–350M. This is the single largest fiscal uncertainty and it is unresolvable from public sources.

  7. Breakeven at $60 is a 20% cushion, not a comfortable one. With Class 4 capex (−30%/+50%) and reserves that could plausibly be −30%, the joint probability of falling below breakeven is not small. A portfolio manager should treat $60 as the central breakeven and $75–80 as the robust one — the price at which the project survives a simultaneous 20% reserve shortfall and 20% capex overrun.
