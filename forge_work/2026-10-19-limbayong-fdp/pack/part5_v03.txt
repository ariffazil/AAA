#heading(level: 1)[5    The Earth]

#text(size: 8.3pt, fill: grey)[The previous revision had no geology. This Part is the object the other Parts are about.]

#heading(level: 2)[5.1    Why this section exists]
A field development plan that contains production, facilities and economics but no geology is a
plan for a factory with no ore body. The earlier revisions of this pack described Limbayong as a
set of volumes and a vessel. That is the accountant's view of a mountain range.

This Part asks a different question: what does the ground actually do? Six things follow from the
answer, and five of them change decisions taken elsewhere in the pack.

#heading(level: 2)[5.2    Plate framework]
The northern margin of Borneo is the product of one long collision and one long escape.

South China Sea oceanic crust was subducting southward beneath Borneo from the Late Eocene. The
sediment stripped off the downgoing plate built an accretionary prism — the Crocker complex, now
exposed on land as folded turbidites. In the early Middle Miocene the attenuated continental crust
of the Dangerous Grounds arrived at the trench and could not be subducted. It collided. The prism
was uplifted and eroded, producing the regional surface that every well in this province
penetrates: the Deep Regional Unconformity. Tan and Lamy (1990) describe this as the change from a
trench-associated basin to a prograding shelf-slope system, and divide the margin into seven
tectonostratigraphic provinces.

Figure 5.1 places Limbayong in that framework. The field is not on the shelf. It sits in the
deepwater fold-and-thrust belt outboard of the shelf edge, in the province the same authors call
the Outboard Belt, between the NW Sabah Trough and the prograding Baram-Champion delta system.

Limbayong sits in the deepwater belt outboard of the shelf, not on the platform. Coastline: Natural Earth 1:50m. Outlines are schematic; position is exact. Sources S-19, S-14. #tag("DERIVED")],

#v(2mm)
#text(size: 8.6pt)[
  Two numbers matter for everything downstream. First, the belt is *young and still moving*: Tan and
  Lamy record a Late Pliocene deformation phase that produced gentle anticlines with numerous
  crestal faults across this very province. Second, the belt is *built of the same sediment the
  reservoirs are made of* — the folds are the delta, shortened. That is why the traps are long,
  narrow and faulted rather than broad and simple.
]

#heading(level: 2)[5.4    Stratigraphy and what the reservoir actually is]
Tan and Lamy divide the margin into four stages, and the division still holds.

#figure(
  image("fig/fig5_strat.png", width: 100%),
  caption: [Figure 5.5 — Tectonostratigraphic column. Stages I–III are deep-marine clastics of the accretionary wedge, truncated by the Deep Regional Unconformity; Stage IV is the prograding shelf-slope system that hosts the deepwater fans. The Kinarut fan (10.6 Ma) is the regional analogue for the reservoir interval, not a stated Limbayong reservoir. After Tan & Lamy (1990) and Cullen et al. (2006). #tag("DERIVED")],
)

#v(2mm)
#text(size: 8.6pt)[
  The reservoir is not a layer cake. The operator's own description is precise: *"vertical
  intercalation of thin-bed, thick sand reservoir"* (S-13). That phrase is worth unpacking, because
  it is the whole engineering problem in eight words.
]
#v(1.5mm)
#text(size: 8.6pt)[
  Thin beds and thick beds interbedded means permeability varies by orders of magnitude over
  metres. Flow will not be homogeneous. Water or gas will find the thick beds first; the thin beds
  will hold oil that the pressure front reaches late or never. Recovery factor in such a system is
  not a property of the field — it is a property of the completion design, the well spacing and the
  pressure support, which is why the operator's paper talks about *"high-grading producer-injector
  pairing"* rather than about a field-wide number.
]

#heading(level: 2)[5.5    The trap, and why one field is really several]
Limbayong is a *severe elongated structure: 30 km long, 2.5 km wide* (S-13). Aspect ratio 12:1.
That is not a dome. It is a ridge, and ridges in thrust belts are cut by cross-faults.

#figure(
  image("fig/fig5_structure.png", width: 100%),
  caption: [Figure 5.4 — Schematic cross-section: a thrust-cored anticline with a gas column over an oil rim, cut into compartments by thrust splays, detached on overpressured shale, with crestal faulting. Not from seismic, and not to scale — topology only. Geometry after published descriptions of the NW Borneo belt (S-13, S-19). #tag("ASSUMED")],
)

#v(2mm)
#text(size: 8.6pt)[
  The operator does not leave this to inference. Their paper names the problem: the structure has
  *"varying faults frequency"* and *"lateral compartmentalization which impend effectiveness of well
  drainage and pressure maintenance"*. It also records that four appraisal wells since 2002 gave
  *"diverse subsurface understanding, indicated possible different depositional model and greater
  degree of complication."*
]
#v(2mm)
#block(fill: rgb("#FDF4F4"), stroke: (left: 3pt + red), inset: 9pt, width: 100%)[
  #set text(size: 8.4pt)
  *This is the most important sentence in the pack, and it is the operator's, not mine.* The company
  that owns the data says the field is compartmentalised in a way that defeats well drainage and
  pressure maintenance. Any development plan that assumes one connected tank — including a
  material-balance model, a single recovery factor, or a single plateau — is contradicted by the
  operator's own published description.
]

#v(2mm)
#text(size: 8.6pt)[
  Put a number on it. Ten development wells across 30 km is one well every 3 km. If the fault
  blocks are 2–5 km wide — the assumption in L-52, and it is an assumption — then several
  compartments receive no well at all, and the wells that exist cannot drain them. The field would
  produce from the compartments it happens to intersect, at a rate set by those compartments, and
  the rest of the 139 MMbbl would sit behind a fault. This is testable, cheaply: interference
  testing and tracers between wells, early. It is also the reason the well count is a bigger value
  lever than the vessel.
]

#heading(level: 2)[5.6    Charge, leakage, and the oil rim]
The discovery history is a geological argument in three dates.

#table(columns: (26mm, 30mm, 1fr),
  table.header([*Date*], [*Event*], [*What it implies*]),
  [early 2000s], [Limbayong-1, gas], [A gas accumulation was found first. Public estimates put it near 800 bcf (S-16).],
  [17 Mar 2014], [Limbayong-2 appraisal: 136 m of oil-bearing sand], [The appraisal found an oil column beneath the gas. Shell 35%, ConocoPhillips 35%, PETRONAS Carigali 30% at the time (S-15).],
  [2026], [FID, 100% PCSB], [The oil rim, not the gas, is what converted a stranded gas discovery into a development.],
)

#v(2mm)
#text(size: 8.6pt)[
  Why is there an oil rim under a gas field at all? The basin-modelling study for the adjacent
  Block R gives a mechanism that fits (S-20): in this system, traps with larger structural relief
  retain oil, while *gas leaks preferentially and progressively to shallower levels, leaving the oil
  behind*. The same paper reports that the leakage model is observed at Kikeh and Gumusut-Kakap, and
  that seabed pockmarks and mud volcanoes in the area indicate hydrocarbons reaching the surface
  today.
]
#v(2mm)
#text(size: 8.6pt)[
  If that mechanism is operating at Limbayong, three consequences follow, and none of them is in the
  previous revisions:
]
#v(1mm)
#set enum(numbering: "1.")
+ *The gas column may be a leaking accumulation, not a sealed one.* 790.9 bcf is a static volumetric
  estimate. On a trap that vents, the producible gas is set by seal capacity and leakage rate, not
  by trap volume.
+ *The oil rim is the resource that stayed.* Which is consistent with it having been missed by the
  first well in 2002 and found by an appraisal eleven years later.
+ *The top seal is a dynamic boundary.* Crestal faults in a Late Pliocene deformation phase are the
  same faults a gas chimney would use. Seal integrity should be tested, not assumed, and any
  development that changes pressure in the gas cap changes the leakage driving force.

#open-banner[
  *Not established:* whether seepage is active at Limbayong specifically. The pockmarks and mud
  volcanoes are documented in the adjacent Block R, on the same trend, not on the Limbayong
  structure. This is a hypothesis with a mechanism and an analogue, not a measurement. It is cheap
  to test: a seabed geochemical survey and a repeat bathymetric or AUV survey over the crest.
]

#heading(level: 2)[5.7    Geomechanics — the Earth pushing back on the drill bit]
The operator has published the drilling problem, which is unusual and useful (S-17):

- the drilling margin is *less than 0.5 ppg* on some wells, even at the surface section;
- most deep wells must be drilled *highly deviated, in the direction of minimum horizontal stress*;
- the consequences named are stuck pipe, hole instability, differential sticking and skin damage;
- managed pressure drilling is required, with a multi-phase model for gas influx transport.

#v(1mm)
#text(size: 8.6pt)[
  A 0.5 ppg window is not a drilling inconvenience. It is a cost and schedule driver, a well-count
  constraint, and a reason why the tenth well may cost more than the first. Any capital estimate
  built from day-rate times days, without an MPD spread and a geomechanical contingency, is
  optimistic at the source. This is also the part of the Earth that most directly supports the
  decision to drill fewer, better-placed wells rather than many.
]

#heading(level: 2)[5.11    In one page]
#block(fill: lightbg, inset: 10pt, width: 100%, stroke: (left: 3pt + navy))[
  #set text(size: 8.5pt)
  Limbayong is not a dome with a vessel attached. It is a *30 km ridge* in an active fold-thrust
  belt, made of the same delta sediment that was shortened to form it, cut by faults whose spacing
  the operator describes as variable and whose effect the operator describes as defeating drainage.
  #v(2pt)
  The reservoir is thin beds and thick beds interbedded — heterogeneous at the scale of metres, not
  kilometres. That is why recovery is a design question and not a number.
  #v(2pt)
  The gas is the phase that leaks in this basin. The oil stayed. An appraisal well found 136 m of
  oil sand eleven years after the discovery well found gas, which is exactly what a leaking gas
  accumulation with an oil rim looks like.
  #v(2pt)
  The drilling margin is under half a pound per gallon on some wells. Every well is expensive and
  every well carries risk. Ten wells over 30 km is three kilometres apart — which may be too far if
  the compartments are two to five kilometres wide.
  #v(2pt)
  And the field is small: 139 MMbbl. What makes it worth building is not the 139. It is the
  800 MMboe of neighbours and the operator's stated intention to be the deepwater hub for East
  Malaysia. *That is the investment case, and it is a different case from the one being told in
  public.*
]
